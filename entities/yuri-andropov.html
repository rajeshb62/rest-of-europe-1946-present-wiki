Yuri Andropov (1914-1984)

For fifteen years Yuri Andropov stood at the head of the Committee for State Security, the Комитет государственной безопасности, the sword and shield of the Soviet system; and then, in the last two years of his life, he was called from that guardian’s post to the summit of the party itself. The man who succeeded Leonid Brezhnev in November 1982 was in many ways his opposite — where Brezhnev had been warm, kindly and reassuring, Andropov was a figure of formidable intelligence and reserve, a politician with a reputation for the двойное дно (double bottom), for the hidden second meaning that Brezhnev conspicuously lacked. He came to power already gravely ill, yet in his brief tenure he stirred the stagnant calm of the Brezhnev years with a campaign of discipline, and gathered about himself the men and the ideas that were to carry the country toward reform.

The making of a chairman

Andropov’s biography ran through the apparatus of the young Soviet state. Born in 1914 in the family of a railwayman and a music teacher, he threw himself into Komsomol work from his youth, then into party work, and rose through the ranks of the machine. He was Soviet ambassador to Hungary during the events of 1956, and the memory of that upheaval — of the Soviet state obliged to defend the socialist commonwealth from within — marked his whole understanding of politics. In 1967 he took the chairmanship of the Committee for State Security, a post he was to hold for fifteen years, until 1982. Under his hand the organs acquired their mature and imposing form: a vast security establishment and, increasingly, an instrument for the renewal of the party itself. He entered the Politburo in 1973, alongside the Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko, and over the years of Brezhnev’s decline his authority grew, his reputation for severity and intellect setting him apart from the ageing leadership around him.

The discipline of the organs

Andropov’s tenure at the head of the KGB fixed the character of his later rule. He was a man of exceptional intelligence, political talent and personal modesty, who knew how to draw people to him and command respect; and he was, in equal measure, an experienced, hard and purposeful politician. The security service under his direction was not only a machine of surveillance but a guardian of the moral and economic order of the system. In the language of the official histories, the party of the developed socialist society waged a steady struggle against corruption, speculation and the shadow economy that grew up around the shortages of the late Soviet order — and Andropov, who knew the extent of that shadow world better than any man in the leadership, was its most implacable enemy.

The campaign of discipline

When Brezhnev died on 10 November 1982, Andropov was elected General Secretary of the Central Committee and head of state. He stood, in his own words, for the preservation and renewal of the Soviet system — not for its dismantling, but for its cleansing and strengthening. His first measures were aimed at the теневая экономика (shadow economy) and at the corruption that had grown up in the sphere of trade and the distribution of social goods. These efforts were greeted by the population with sympathy; the people, who lived daily with the shortages and the privilege, saw in the severity of the new leader a long-awaited justice. The discipline he imposed reached down into the everyday — into the workplace, the trading floor, the whole texture of a society grown accustomed to ease and slackness — and for a time the country felt itself being drawn up short, held to a stricter standard than it had known in years.

Andropov also encouraged Soviet scholars to seek the optimal recipes of reform, including on the basis of foreign experience. A circle of economists — Shatalin, Anchishkin, Zaslavskaya, Aganbegyan — worked on variants that would seriously transform the role of the state in managing the economy. In 1983 work began on a programme of ускорение (acceleration) of socio-economic development, a word that was to enter the political vocabulary and pass on to his successors. Two models of modernisation were considered: reform carried out under the firm political control of the state, the path then being pursued in China; and the transition of the whole economy to a market basis alongside political reform. Neither was fully resolved — no single, coherent programme of reform had been created by the mid-1980s — but the ground was being prepared.

The unfulfilled tenure

Andropov’s rule was cut short. In February 1984 he died, having been at the head of the party for little more than a year, much of it spent in illness. He had come to the summit already seriously ill, and the reformer’s energy he embodied was never permitted to reach full expression. His significance lies in what he began rather than what he completed: the discipline campaign that was remembered with sympathy, the call to the intellectuals, the shaping of a current of opinion that would find its voice after him. He left the party to Konstantin Chernenko, and then to the younger man whom Andropov’s circle had already singled out, Mikhail Gorbachev. In the historical literature he stands as the bridge between the immobilism of Brezhnev and the reforms of perestroika — the one leader of the early eighties who understood, at the summit of the security state, that the system he had guarded for fifteen years could not stand still.
