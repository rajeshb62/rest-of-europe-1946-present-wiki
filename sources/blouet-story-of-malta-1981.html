Brian Blouet — The Story of Malta (revised edition, 1981)

A one-volume historical geography of the Maltese islands, in eight chapters from the limestone sandwich of the rocks and the Neolithic temples, through the Arab, Norman and Aragonese centuries, the rule of the Order of St. John (1530–1798) and the Great Siege of 1565, to the British century and a half (1800–1964), the siege of the Second World War, independence in 1964, the republic of 1974 and the departure of the last British forces in March 1979.

Scope in this KB. The relevant part is Chapter 8 (pp. 211–222) and the end of Chapter 7: postwar reconstruction and the £30 million war-damage fund, the 1947 return of self-government, the 1950s Labour–Nationalist alternation, the integration-with-Britain referendum, the suspension of 1959, the Blood Commission, independence on 21 September 1964, the defence agreements of 1964 and 1972, the republic of 13 December 1974 and the withdrawal of British forces in March 1979. Earlier chapters give the long background and are also filed in the wikis for 1800–1865, 1866–1919 and 1919–45.

Summary

Brian Blouet, a geographer at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln who first worked on Malta under the Order for a higher degree (1960–64), wrote The Story of Malta (Faber and Faber, 1967; revised 1972, 1976; this revised edition printed and published in Malta by Progress Press, 1981) as a continuous history in which the physical setting, the settlement pattern and the population count carry much of the argument. Malta, he says, lacks natural resources, is short of water and is small, yet “has never been very far from the centre of events” because it lies in the narrows joining the eastern and western Mediterranean, can hold a garrison provided it is fed from outside, and has the deep-water Grand Harbour and Marsamxett. Malta flourishes militarily when it is held by a power other than the one holding Sicily.

About two-fifths of the book belongs to the Order of St. John (Chapters 4 and 5, pp. 46–135): the Great Siege, the foundation of Valletta, the growth of the harbour towns, population estimates from militia lists and parish status animarum returns, cotton, corsairing, slavery, and the Order’s decline until Napoleon takes Malta in June 1798. Chapter 6, “Malta and Britain” (pp. 136–191), carries the British period down to 1939: the French occupation and the Maltese rising of September 1798, the blockade and the British takeover of 1800, the Treaty of Amiens, the trade boom of the Napoleonic Wars, the commissions of 1812 and 1836, the governors, the Crimean War and the Suez Canal, the constitutions of 1849, 1887, 1903 and 1921, the language question, the church-and-state crisis of the 1930s and the economy between the wars. Chapter 7 treats the Axis air siege of 1940–43; Chapter 8 the post-war constitutions, the integration proposal of the 1950s, independence on 21 September 1964, and the Labour government’s renegotiation of the defence agreement.

Blouet stresses throughout that Malta’s economy was dependent on outside money, first the Order’s European estates and then British naval and military spending; that politics were dominated by a small group of lawyers, clergy and landowners; and that the contested question of language (Italian, English or Maltese) was bound up with class and with the Church. He is openly critical of the way chroniclers wrote the 1565 Siege, and compares it with accounts of the 1941–42 siege.

Contents

Preface (p. 9)

Records how the book grew from the author’s 1960–64 degree work on Malta under the Order, which scholarship it draws on (Luttrell’s Medieval Malta, Edith Dobie, Hilda Lee, Godfrey Wettinger’s fifteenth-century articles), and the debt to the University of Malta, the Bodleian’s Melitensia (the Hannibal Scicluna collection), Durham and Nebraska. He observes that much modern Maltese history is written from the archives of the two overlords, the Order and the British.

Chapter 1 — The Geographical Setting (pp. 11–20)

The islands (Malta, Gozo, Comino, Cominotto, Filfla) cover 122 square miles, less than the Isle of Wight. Their strategic value is positional, but Pantelleria and Lampedusa are as well placed; Malta has the size to hold a garrison and the Grand Harbour. The rock is a “sandwich”: upper coralline limestone, greensand, blue clay, globigerina limestone and lower coralline limestone. The blue clay carries the springs and fertile soil; the soft globigerina is the building stone that hardens in air; the north-west is broken into ridges and depressions by faulting, with the Great Fault forming the escarpment on which defence has always concentrated, leaving the north-west exposed to raiders. Climate: summer drought, mild wet winters, the xlokk (sirocco) and the grigal (north-east wind that can close the harbours), average rainfall of 22 inches that varies from under 10 to 40. Man arrived about 6,000 years ago to a wooded land and stripped soil and vegetation, creating barren karst and increasing aridity.

Chapter 2 — Malta in Prehistory and Antiquity (pp. 21–34)

Neolithic farmers from Sicily from about 5000 BC (Ghar Dalam, Grey and Red Skorba phases), then the Temple Culture from about 4100 BC (Zebbug, Mgarr, Ggantija, Saflieni, Tarxien): free-standing megalithic temples, the Hal Saflieni hypogeum with the remains of over 6,000 burials, “fat lady” sculptures. Blouet discusses the unexplained questions (who built the temples, a possible clan workforce of roughly 4,000 people divided into six territories, the collapse probably linked to soil exhaustion and drying climate), the Tarxien Cemetery, Borg in-Nadur and Bahrija Bronze Age peoples, and the cart-tracks cut into the rock. After Phoenician and Carthaginian settlement from the eighth century BC, Rome takes the island in 218 BC (Second Punic War); Roman Malta is prosperous and not fully Romanised, and the Acts of the Apostles (St Paul’s shipwreck, AD 60) call the islanders barbaroi.

Chapter 3 — Medieval Malta (pp. 35–45)

After Vandal raids and a Byzantine period, the Arabs conquer the islands from Sicily in AD 870. Maltese is of Arabic origin and so are most place-names. Roger the Norman takes Malta in 1090 and Roger II again in 1127; Blouet quotes Luttrell that “Malta was never really Norman at all.” Under Frederick II after about 1220 Western influence grows; the 1241 count records 1,119 families (836 Muslim, 250 Christian, 33 Jewish) and Blouet estimates about 5,000 people against Luttrell’s 10,000. Church building, the shift from grain and stock to cotton and cumin for export, the fifteenth-century desertion of small villages, the 1419 militia list, the Moorish raid of 1429 and the rise of a Sicilian-Aragonese landowning caste whose Romance names contrast with the Arabic-named majority, a division Blouet links to the later language question. By 1528 the islands hold about 20,000 people, and the commissioners of the Order judged them poorly for military reasons.

Chapter 4 — The Knights of St. John and the Great Siege of Malta (pp. 46–68)

The Order, expelled from Rhodes in 1523, accepts Charles V’s offer of Malta and Tripoli in 1530 (annual fee of one falcon; eight langues, the English one soon defunct), settling in Birgu and Fort St. Angelo. The Turkish raid of July 1551 depopulates Gozo (some 5,000 taken into slavery) and Tripoli falls; the Order builds Forts St. Elmo and St. Michael. Jean de la Valette (elected 1557) prepares for the attack; Don Garcia de Toledo, Viceroy of Sicily, predicts it. The Turkish fleet of about 200 ships lands at Marsaxlokk on 18–19 May 1565; Blouet argues that attacking St. Elmo was tactically defensible, that the fort was modern, that Mustapha and Piali’s alleged quarrels are overtold, and that the Turks wasted men by assaulting before breaches were ready. St. Elmo falls on 23 June after about a month; Dragut is mortally wounded; the assault on St. Michael fails on 15 July; the cavalry raid from Mdina on the Marsa camp, the mined bastion of 18 August, and Don Garcia’s relief force landing on 6 September end the siege. He defends Don Garcia against the knights’ criticism, doubts the 30,000–40,000 figure for the Turkish army (20,000 or less is likelier), stresses that over 5,000 of about 9,000 defenders were Maltese, and judges that 1565 was “an important battle in European history but not a decisive one,” yet the foundation year of Malta’s modern history.

Chapter 5 — Malta under the Order of St. John (pp. 69–135)

Aftermath: Valletta begun on 28 March 1566 (Laparelli’s plan; the Order moves in on 18 March 1571; regulations of 1569 on building; water by Wignacourt’s aqueduct in 1614). Population from the 1528 commission and the status animarum: about 20,000 in 1530; 28,864 in 1590; 41,084 in 1614; about 50,000 in 1670–80 (plague of 1676); 87,749 in 1784 and 96,534 in 1797, so roughly fivefold growth over the Order’s rule, with Valletta and the Three Cities (Birgu, Senglea, Bormla) holding 34 per cent of the people in 1590 and 38 per cent (with Floriana, built from the 1720s) in 1797. Villages grow around the 1436 parishes while small hamlets die; coastal towers and forts are built by Wignacourt, Lascaris and de Redin; Mdina and the Gozo Castello decline.

Finance: income from the commanderies’ responsions, the spoglie, entry fees and prizes (about 208,000 scudi in 1587; 1,364,174 in 1780), foundations by Grand Masters, the galley squadron and the shipyards, the sixteenth- to eighteenth-century cotton industry, windmills, salt, the corso (privateering), slavery (about 2,000 slaves for most of the eighteenth century), Sicilian grain rights and famine years, and field-building on rock. The Order in decline: no empire (West Indian islands bought in 1653, sold 1665; Corsica lost to France in 1763), a 1775 priests’ revolt, French confiscation of its estates in 1792, Napoleon’s seizure on 10–12 June 1798, the capitulation terms and the expulsion of the Order. Blouet concludes that the Order’s investments in harbours and fortifications “insured the prosperity of the islands for another century and a half” by attracting British naval power.

Chapter 6 — Malta and Britain (pp. 136–191)

French rule and the British takeover (1798–1802). Napoleon abolishes the nobility, strips the Church and leaves General Vaubois with about 4,000 troops. The Maltese rise at Mdina on 2 September 1798, form a National Assembly, and with Captain Alexander Ball (Civil Commissioner), General Graham and Nelson blockade the French in Valletta; Vaubois capitulates on 5 September 1800. British policy is undecided; Cameron succeeds Ball; a Maltese deputation to London in 1802 fails; Article 10 of the Treaty of Amiens restores the Order, but Britain stays. Nelson comes to see Malta as “a most important outwork to India.” The Berlin and Milan decrees, the Continental System and the licensing system make Malta an entrepôt (the “period of the great trade”), ended by the plague of 1813.

Commissions and governors. The 1812 commission recommends crown-colony government and rejects the revival of the Consiglio Popolare; Sir Thomas Maitland (“King Tom”) becomes first Governor in 1813, abolishes the old Università, promotes English; Britain’s possession is recognised in 1814. Economic depression follows until the 1840s, with the cotton industry collapsing and cholera in 1837 (over 4,000 dead). Camillo Sciberras and George Mitrovich press for reform, the Council of Government (appointed) is set up in 1835, and the commission of John Austin and George Cornwall Lewis in 1836 brings a free press and schooling but no elected assembly. Under More O’Ferrall (1847–51) the 1849 constitution adds eight elected members to a Council with an official majority; Italian refugees and the 1848 revolutions make the fortress seem at risk. Sir William Reid (1851–58) governs with the elected members, introduces experiments (plough, cattle, Indian silkworms) and supports the Crimean War boom and harbour expansion; Gaspard Le Marchant (1858–64) reasserts the Governor’s authority over army commanders; the Cardwell principle (1864) says money votes would not be pressed against elected members. Adrian Dingli, Crown Advocate and then Chief Justice, is called the unofficial governor.

Politics to 1919. The 1887 constitution gives the elected members a majority and control of finances; a reform-minded pro-English group (Savona) faces an anti-reform pro-Italian group (Fortunato Mizzi); Gerald Strickland as Chief Secretary (1889–96) is blocked on taxes, water supply and sewage even after the 1887 cholera (about 800 deaths). In 1901 an order in council overrides the Council on schools, drainage and waterworks; the Hydraulic Dock Company fails when the Council refuses to guarantee interest; the 1903 constitution restores an official majority, a position unchanged until 1921. Blouet describes “mandarin government” by the Governor, Chief Justice, Bishop and department heads, notes that around 1900 only some 10,000 of nearly 200,000 inhabitants had the vote, and sketches the Church’s relations with British governors (the see separated from Palermo in 1831, Gozo bishopric in 1863, the 1889 insistence that the Bishop be Maltese).

Economic change. British defence spending rises from the 1840s (garrison, bakery, dockyard, first Admiralty dry dock 1848; military spending about £200,000 a year at the end of the 1840s, over £800,000 in 1856). The Crimean War boom causes harbour congestion; Reid and Le Marchant move the Royal Navy to French Creek and build commercial quays at Marsa. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 puts Malta on the route to India: the Somerset Dock (1871), the hydraulic dock, Hamilton Dock (1892), the Victoria Lines along the Great Fault in the 1870s and the Grand Harbour breakwater of 1907–09. By 1880 Malta is the chief coaling station, with over 3,000 steamers entering that year, though trade peaks in the late 1880s and declines as engines become more efficient and Tunis and Algiers take coaling. The cotton industry falls from 25 per cent of the workforce in 1842 to 2.5 per cent in 1901. Rural life described through the 1836 evidence (labourers on about sixpence a day).

Population, settlement, transport, education. First census 1842: 112,500. Growth over 1 per cent a year in the 1880s; density about 2,000 a square mile by 1931. Emigration, mainly to North Africa (over 50,000 Maltese around the Mediterranean by 1900) and, after the First World War, to Australia, the United States, Canada and Britain. Suburbs (Sliema, Gzira, Pieta, Hamrun, Pawla) grow around the harbours. The Malta Railway ran from Valletta to Mdina (opened 1883) and closed on 1 April 1931; electric trams began in 1905. Education: Italian as the language of instruction against the Keenan report of 1879 (Maltese as base, English second); compulsory attendance acts only in 1924 and 1946; in the 1920s 90 per cent of prospective emigrants were found illiterate.

1900–1939. Dockyard employment peaks at over 9,000 Maltese in 1905 and falls to about 5,000 by 1911 (royal commission of 1911); wartime price inflation; a dockyard strike in 1917; riots in Valletta on 7 June 1919 in which three Maltese were shot dead and a fourth died later. Lord Plumer (1919–24) combines firmness with relief (bread subsidy, public works). L. S. Amery’s diarchic 1921 constitution gives a Legislative Assembly and a Senate of nominated interests, a single-transferable-vote franchise for men over 21, and Joseph Howard becomes first Prime Minister; the Labour Party (founded 12 April 1921), the Constitutional Party of Gerald (later Lord) Strickland, Enrico Mizzi’s pro-Italian Partito Nazionalista Democratico and the Unione Politica compete. In 1930 the Archbishop and the Bishop of Gozo forbid votes for Strickland and the constitution is suspended; a royal commission in 1931 makes Maltese the language of the courts; the 1932 election restores the constitution and a Nationalist majority that moves to keep Italian in elementary schools; the constitution is suspended again in 1933 and Maltese made the court language by letters patent in 1934. Blouet reads the language question as a social barrier (Italian the language of lawyers, priests and the nobility) and as a question of loyalty. Governor Bonham-Carter (1936–40) rebuilds relations; the Italian consul’s espionage is stopped, the Istituto di Cultura Italiana closed; Chief Justice Mercieca is confined in 1940 and exiled to Uganda in 1942. Attempts to diversify the economy between the wars (tourist bureau 1924) mostly fail.

Chapter 7 — The Second World War (pp. 192–210)

Blouet compares the siege of 1940–43 with 1565 and again with the knights’ own doubts. Before the war the Royal Air Force thought Malta indefensible by air (an 800-lb bomb experiment on Comino), the Royal Navy wanted it kept, and so little was prepared that the fleet withdrew to Alexandria. Italy attacks on 11 June 1940; the three Gladiators “Faith, Hope and Charity” hold off the Regia Aeronautica until Hurricanes arrive. Malta-based aircraft, submarines (Upholder under Wanklyn) and, from May 1941, Air Marshal Lloyd’s reorganised strike force sink Axis supply ships to North Africa (over 60 per cent lost in November 1941). The German Fliegerkorps X reaches Sicily in December 1940–January 1941; in February–April 1942 bombing rises from over 1,000 to 6,700 tons a month; Spitfires flown from carriers and Keith Park’s interception tactics turn the air battle in mid-1942; the planned Axis invasion is cancelled; the Santa Marija convoy arrives in August 1942. Civil defence (rock-cut shelters for 165,900 by May 1941), evacuation, near-starvation (1,500–1,609 calories a day for workers in 1942), 1,490 civilian deaths, 35,000 homes destroyed or damaged, the George Cross awarded on 15 April 1942, Roosevelt’s citation in 1943 and the Allied use of the island as a base for the invasion of Sicily in 1943. A £30 million war-damage fund follows.

Chapter 8 — Independence (pp. 211–222)

War changed social patterns. Sir Wilfred Woods (1945) and Balogh and Seers advised on diversification; dockyard layoffs began in 1949; a five-year plan (1959–64) planned £32,250,000, of which £22 million from Britain. Internal self-government returns on 5 September 1947 (unicameral, universal suffrage including women; Labour under Paul Boffa wins 24 of 40 seats; Dom Mintoff becomes the rising Labour figure). A Boffa–Mintoff split in 1949 over dockyard dismissals, Nationalist governments under Mizzi and Borg Olivier in 1950–55, Mintoff’s Labour government 1955–58 and an integration-with-the-United-Kingdom proposal that lapsed after a referendum with a low turn-out; the constitution is suspended in 1959; the Blood Commission (1960–61) leads to a restored constitution and the 1962 election won by the Nationalists. Independence is granted on 21 September 1964 within the Commonwealth with a defence agreement worth £50 million over ten years; tourism expands after 1966; in 1967 Britain announces cuts to its overseas military spending. Mintoff’s Labour government (from June 1971) renegotiates: £14 million a year from the United Kingdom and NATO for 1972–79; Malta becomes a republic on 13 December 1974; the last British forces leave in March 1979. Blouet closes by noting Malta’s dense population and land-use problems and hoping “independence and neutrality” will last.

Appendices and Bibliography (pp. 223–232 and back matter)

Appendix I lists the Grand Masters of the Order in Malta, 1530–1798 (L’Isle-Adam to Hompesch). Appendix II lists Civil Commissioners, Governors, Governors-General and Presidents (Ball 1799 to Dorman and Mamo; Buttigieg from 1976). Appendix III lists the Prime Ministers of Malta from Joseph Howard (1921) to Mintoff. The selected bibliography is arranged by chapter and cites, among others, Bosio, Riley-Smith, Luttrell (ed.), Medieval Malta, Trump, Evans, Renfrew, Galea’s bibliography of the Great Siege, Thornton’s memoir on the Order’s finances, Laferla’s British Malta, Hilda Lee, Edith Dobie, Jeremy Boissevain, and Bowen-Jones, Dewdney and Fisher’s Malta: Background for Development. The inside back cover carries reviews of earlier editions.

Notes for use

  • Perspective. A geographer’s account, strongest on population, settlement, trade and the economy, written for a general reader. Blouet gives his own estimates against those of Luttrell and Wettinger and flags where numbers are uncertain.
  • Chronicles criticised. He warns that the Siege story was written by Western European chroniclers glorifying the Order, that Turkish accounts are little known, and that the Maltese contribution is neglected.
  • Extraction. Printed page numbers appear as stand-alone lines; maps and graphs reduce to scattered labels, and the tables in Chapters 3 and 5 are partly scrambled.
