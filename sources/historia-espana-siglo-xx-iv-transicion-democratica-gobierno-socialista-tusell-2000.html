Content Summary

Front matter. Series blurb for the four-volume Historia de España en el siglo XX — a contested century whose knowledge is indispensable for any diagnosis of the present; Spain protagonist of two fundamental events (Civil War and Transition); Spanish culture from the early twentieth century at heights permitting comparison with the Siglo de Oro. Volume IV covers “un periodo fundamental para la España actual: la transición democrática y el primer gobierno socialista.” Title page: Javier Tusell / Historia de España en el siglo XX / IV. La transición democrática y el gobierno socialista; ePUB r1.3 (JeSsE 06.02.14); original title note; Javier Tusell, 2000; digital-editor credits.

La transición a la democracia (1975–1982)

Opens by placing Spain’s twentieth-century world role as relatively reduced, with two exceptions: the Civil War of 1936–1939 (battlefield of Europe’s antagonisms more than Spanish protagonism) and the Transition to democracy. Historians, like Vargas Llosa’s intellectuals, are often “amantes de las catástrofes,” so the Civil War has drawn more ink than a process that ended well; objective importance of 1975–1982 is not thereby reduced. No near models, local or temporal, suggested that such a process could succeed without external aggression.

El comienzo de la «tercera ola»: Las transiciones hacia la democracia en el Mediterráneo

The Spanish case framed in Huntington’s “third wave” of democratizations. The wave began in the Mediterranean in 1974 and removed the non-totalitarian right-wing dictatorships of Greece, Portugal and Spain; it then crossed the Atlantic and, unpredictably, reached the communist regimes and the Soviet Union itself. Transitions explained in terms of profitability for all political actors when the cost of repression or violent insurrection is perceived as excessive; democratic consensus appears when institutions become “what unites us all” (Thiers). Every transition is a multi-sided chess game whose final result is not written; individuals and circumstance matter.

La peculiaridad de la vía española a la democracia

A quarter-century on, the Spanish case can be treated as the paradigmatic, exemplary or canonical transition. Complete, without Gorbachev-style path uncertainty and without Pinochet-style “authoritarian enclaves”; the political class converted sincerely to democratic principles rather than fictitiously adapting. Decisive prior transformation under the dictatorship (without parallel in Greece, Portugal, Turkey, or even fascist Italy) made the outcome possible. Result of given circumstances and of individual and collective protagonists.

La nueva monarquía: El rey Juan Carlos I

The monarchical institution as a distinctive Spanish trait of the Transition. Its function was not decorative: the great danger in any transition is the break of legitimacy between the vanished dictatorship and the not-yet-consolidated democracy; the Monarchy supplied an instrument to avoid that break, without parallel in other third-wave cases. From the first moment Juan Carlos chose to be a great king rather than a small Caudillo.

Arias Navarro o el reformismo imposible

The King’s early need to establish supreme responsibility and obtain the resignation of the president of the government; appointment of Torcuato Fernández Miranda as President of the Cortes and of the Council of the Kingdom. Arias Navarro’s reformism impossible from the start; Fraga’s and Areilza’s roles; the government as “colchón” (Ossorio) between two periods.

La crisis del semestre Arias

Essential difficulties from the incoherence of the government and the limitations of its president, compounded by public-order conflicts, economic crisis and the wave of strikes of early 1976 (especially Madrid). Political demands alone could not mobilise mass protest, yet a sense of release among organised workers extended the conflictivity. Arias’s reformism proved impossible from its origins; yet the Arias period mattered as a cushion between two phases. Substitution engineered through Fernández Miranda’s control of the Council of the Kingdom; tern of Silva Muñoz, Suárez and López de Letona; Fraga and Areilza surprised and indignant.

Adolfo Suárez: el cambio empieza a hacerse realidad

Choice of Suárez among the three young reformist ministers explained by biographical traits optimal for getting franquista political class approval of a substantial Law for Political Reform through the National Council and the Cortes. Judgment on Suárez has shifted sharply within a quarter-century; there are two Suárezes separated by the cardinal date of 1979. Mere origin, charm and skill are insufficient to explain the historical protagonist.

La ley para la reforma política

Fernández Miranda’s path “de la ley a la ley.” Summer 1976 drafting of the Law for Political Reform (Fernández Miranda and Lavilla’s team, especially Herrero); several redactions. 8 September presentation to senior military commanders, to whom Suárez gave the impression that the PCE would not be admitted to legality; neither the King nor he then thought that politically possible, and the party’s statutes seemed to bar it juridically. Referendum approval of the Law.

La transición entre el terrorismo y el golpe militar

From the referendum on the Law for Political Reform to the June 1977 elections: weeks in which terrorism and military pressure could have aborted the process. Spanish will for democratic coexistence, the attitude of party leaders and of the Government prevented involution. Suárez’s best moment in measuring time for two stages of reform — first the Law through the regime’s Cortes, then free elections for a constituent work. Economic crisis constant backdrop; deliberate delay of a harsh economic programme that might have derailed the political calendar (Ossorio’s memoirs).

La legalización del PCE

Atocha massacre of labour lawyers (24 January 1977) — seven dead — produced solidarity and demonstrated the PCE’s capacity to control its masses. Legalisation on 9 April 1977; real tolerance already from December 1976. Carrillo’s return from exile (February 1976), hidden, opening contacts with the opposition.

El nacimiento de un sistema de partidos

Public attention shifted from transition difficulties to the imminent electoral campaign. New party system configured mainly from opposition forces to the previous regime rather than from the dictatorship’s associationism; unitary opposition organisms lost the initiative once Suárez reached the presidency. Spectacle of numerous groups in the months before the elections.

La devolución de España

Julián Marías’s phrase on the “return” of Spain in the Transition; intellectual and journalistic framing of the recovery of a country long held apart from its European peers.

Un largo camino hacia la Constitución

Constituent work after the 1977 elections; consensus politics; long drafting path toward the constitutional text.

La Constitución de 1978: elaboración y contenido

Constitution of eleven titles and 169 articles (plus additional and transitional dispositions). Elaboration by consensus among the principal parliamentary forces; content settling the form of the State, rights, the Crown, Cortes, government, judiciary, territorial organisation (Title VIII), constitutional reform.

La gran cuestión pendiente: nacionalismos y terrorismo

Nationalisms and terrorism as the great unresolved question beside the constitutional settlement; Basque and Catalan tracks; ETA pressure on the new democracy.

Las elecciones de 1979

Second general elections under the new system; consolidation and early strains of the Suárez majority.

Triunfo y caída de Adolfo Suárez

First error of Suárez already in the investiture debate; decline accelerated from 1980. From summer 1980 polls placed Felipe González and the PSOE ahead. Personal and party exhaustion after the constituent years.

Los enemigos de la transición: el papel del Ejército

Military as principal structural enemy of the Transition; successive conspiracies and pressures; the regime’s armed forces as inheritance that the new monarchy and civilian government had to manage without rupture.

El golpe de Estado del 23-F

23 February 1981 coup attempt; Tejero in the Congress; King’s televised intervention; defeat of the conspiracy and its effect on democratic legitimacy (dissatisfaction with the democratic regime falling from about 9 per cent before February 1981 to about 5 per cent by the 1982 elections).

El gobierno Calvo-Sotelo

Calvo-Sotelo after Suárez: collaboration without personal similarity; origin in business and “juanista” monarchist milieu, close to the Catholic family of the regime and to Europeanist sectors; prior procurador in Franco’s Cortes by the sindical third. Inheritance of Suárez including party presidency questions.

La crisis del centrismo

Decline of Calvo-Sotelo’s government parallel to the decomposition of UCD — each unintelligible without the other. A party that had played a transcendental role in the Transition practically ended its life in the 1982 parliamentary election — exceptional in comparative transition history. UCD not simply a vehicle whose only reason for being was the Transition (contrast Adenauer’s CDU); Spanish consensus hard to achieve, so the governing party filtered conflicts internally and avoided confrontation during the constitutional period. After Suárez’s resignation from party presidency, sterile Palma de Mallorca congress (January 1981); 23-F postponed divergences; divorce law and the “plataforma moderada” of some forty centrist deputies (Alzaga, Herrero); Galician elections of October 1981 as first significant region where AP overtook UCD.

El camino del PSOE hacia el Gobierno

Agony of UCD opened the opportunity for the PSOE as second electoral force and logical government alternative. Decisive transformation in the two 1979 congresses — from theoretically radical positions to reformism connecting better with majority Spanish society; Spanish factors in the internal fights beyond the European socialist pattern. Felipe González’s proposal to drop Marxism as exclusive ideological principle; XVIII congress chaos (“gran novatada”); González’s resignation after re-election; only Bustelo willing to succeed him among the radicals; extraordinary congress of September 1979 restored González with a reduced, controllable delegate body.

Octubre del 82: el final de la transición

October 1982 general elections as the political end of the Transition. Institutionally the Transition had ended with the Constitution (December 1978); politically it ends in October 1982 for three main reasons: the political group that had held the protagonist role not only lost power but disappeared, replaced; the military conspiracy had been defeated in 1981 and dissatisfaction with democracy fell; the results themselves became a plebiscite consolidating the regime.

El fin del aislamiento: Política exterior durante la transición

Interior politics predominated, so external transition was slower — not definitive until 1986 — yet democratisation equated to Europeanisation and homologation with the Western world. External factors influenced but did not decide (contrast Portugal). Socialist models Sweden or Austria rather than clear Western-bloc alignment; disappointment at the slowness of EEC entry.

Evolución económica durante la transición política

Politics has dominated the preceding pages; economy now treated at medium term. Calvo-Sotelo’s memoir claim that he governed at the worst moment of the economic crisis. Spanish economy still appeared buoyant in 1973 (8 per cent growth; unemployment barely over 1 per cent; inflation 11 per cent) but structural problems underlay the surface; small public sector yet state omnipresence. Oil shocks overlapped with the political transition; Moncloa Pacts as political pact with economic content.

El cambio social: la transición sindical

Social evolution requires a longer span than seven years, yet demography was already European (intercensal growth 1.1 per cent); first step toward a stationary society; abrupt fertility decline in a still relatively young population. Union transition and the reorganisation of labour representation.

La cultura durante la transición: El tiempo de las recuperaciones

No exact coincidence between political Transition and cultural transition. Late dictatorship already showed cultural belligerence against the political system (e.g. El espíritu de la colmena, La prima Angélica; novels published abroad). Political freedom did not produce an immediate flowering of values the previous situation had marginalised; Spanish culture continued to be led mainly by those who had begun to play their roles earlier. Cultural transition later in time; recovery as keyword.

Un balance de la transición

Positive aspects predominate clearly — not only because the Transition ended well, but because the starting point did not justify full optimism despite economic development. Among third-wave countries, only the Mediterranean starters consolidated democracy rapidly; Spain’s difficulties were comparatively greater than Greece’s or Portugal’s, yet traumas of all kinds were lesser. Spain never passed through the danger of becoming a left-wing dictatorial regime of the Portuguese revolutionary type.

El gobierno socialista (1982–1996)

Shift of the historian’s focus from “tiempo presente” (lived as memory and experience) to “Historia inmediata” — possible but provisional, reconstructing roots of the present in the past without knowing yet how deep they run.

Felipe González, como protagonista histórico

Figure and political personality of Felipe González give unity and coherence to the period; the label “felipismo” has acquired a plainly negative connotation and is set aside. Object of successive enthusiasms and hatreds, often from the same persons. Leader of the party at thirty-two, president of the government at forty — representative of a new political generation, the very counterfigure of Franco (Haro’s “primera cara del antifranquismo”; Vázquez Montalbán’s “antifranquismo inocente”). JOC and HOAC militancy; Jiménez Fernández as critical teacher. Leadership over the party, conquered in 1974 and revalidated in 1979, not seriously contested until the 1996 electoral defeat. Overload of radical ideology from opposition years superimposed by pragmatism that chose moderate reformism in fundamental decisions. First Spanish democratic leader adapted to ordinary democratic work in times of normality; far below Azaña’s intellectual capacity, knew better than Azaña how to set calendar, priorities and content of a reforming action once in power.

Los gobiernos socialistas y la opinión durante la década de los ochenta

Comfortable parliamentary majority; governing team essentially durable through the 1980s. Ministers in three groups; economic portfolios always in hands of specialists long linked to the Administration (e.g. Ministry of Commerce) — greatest continuity with the past, explaining that the essential line of economic policy did not change.

La oposición política y social

Essential factor of political stability: impotence of the opposition, especially the right-wing alternative. AP, animated by having substituted and then liquidated UCD, believed a bipartisan system was configured in which it would be the natural turn; limitations of the right’s electoral offer confirmed in elections and in memoirs of its own leaders.

Un modo de consolidación democrática

Arrival of the Socialists met genuine yearnings for governmental stability and a very negative judgment of UCD. Involucionist dangers disappeared during the Socialist stage; a peculiar political system settled with clearly positive and more ambivalent aspects. No truly significant extreme-right group, unlike other countries of the geographic area; democratic legitimacy already consolidated.

Terrorismo y antiterrorismo: los GAL y sus consecuencias

Antiterrorist policy of the Socialist governments often judged one of their greatest failures, but that judgment born mainly from the medium-term damage of the GAL (Grupos Antiterroristas de Liberación). GAL do not summarise the whole of government action in this field; negative judgment rests on broader grounds. Starting point October 1982 and the positive balance left by Rosón at the Interior Ministry.

La construcción del estado de las autonomías

During the Socialist tenure, development of Title VIII of the Constitution, whose concrete content had still to be specified; repeated earlier attempts to order the autonomic process had failed. Only five Statutes of Autonomy approved by 1981. August 1983 Constitutional Court struck down decisive parts of the LOAPA, discarding any “reconducción” of the autonomic process toward lower competence levels; the Court attributed to itself the power that politicians had been denied, but by that procedure at least a framework was reached.

La política y la realidad económica en la etapa socialista

Economic situation at the Socialists’ arrival as lamentable as earlier pages indicated; second oil shock overlapped the first without political conditions for a coherent durable response. Investment retreating; unemployment already high. Campaign promises of a different economic policy not carried out and apparently never on the real horizon of government purposes — technicians who executed economic policy had been formed in continuity with prior administration.

Hacia la pérdida de la mayoría absoluta (1989–1993)

First half of the 1980s: PSOE voting intention 30–40 per cent; satisfaction with González extremely high (46 to over 60 per cent); right averaging no more than 15 per cent of voting intention before proper campaign periods. 1986–1992 still high PSOE intention (24–33 per cent); decisive opinion change from 1991, when PSOE began definitively to lose the hegemony it had enjoyed. By 1991, 56 per cent of those surveyed thought they paid too much tax relative to services received (91 per cent among senior executives); identification with centre-left declining.

España al final de la época socialista: la sociedad

Important social changes whose origin lay in the earliest moments of the Transition; against the critique of the Transition as a purely political phenomenon. Mid-1990s Spain eighth in the world by GDP and ninth by human development index; population approaching forty million, possibly a ceiling. Demographers already forecasting eventual negative growth between 2010 and 2025.

Nuevas perspectivas culturales

Culture under the Socialist decade and a half: diffusion of education and knowledge; quasi-federal territorial organisation of the State. Gravest problem of Spanish culture at the moment of writing: making real plurality compatible with no less evident unity, or at least very close relation among its components. Development of the State of the Autonomies followed by cultural and linguistic pluralisation.

Relationship to the Wiki

Tusell IV gives this wiki its continuous scholarly history of Spain from Franco’s death to the end of the long Socialist tenure. It continues directly from volume III (La dictadura de Franco), also held here, whose closing chapter on Spain at Franco’s death is the immediate prelude. Beside the de Gaulle Discours et Messages tomes (T2–T5), which supply the French case in the General’s own voice from the Fourth Republic through April 1969, Tusell IV supplies the Spanish case for the generation after the dictatorship — institutions, parties, coup and consolidation, autonomies, terrorism, economy and culture — as modern secondary synthesis.
