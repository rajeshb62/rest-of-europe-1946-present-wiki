The same volume is also held in the Rest of Europe 1919–45 wiki (the argument opens in 1939); it is retained here because the bulk of the narrative after 1945–51 and the whole of the 1951–75 arc fall inside this folder’s span, and because volume IV of the series continues directly from Franco’s death into the Transition

Javier Tusell wrote the volume as a full-scale scholarly synthesis of the Franco regime — the dictator, the regime as a dictatorship, the repression and exile of 1939, the fascist temptation and wartime foreign policy of 1939–45, the postwar isolation and autarky of the 1940s, the years of consensus and economic opening (1951–65), and the tardofranquismo of 1966–75. The author’s generation, as the closing biography notes, was obsessed with why democracy failed under the Second Republic and what the Franco dictatorship and the later restoration under Juan Carlos meant for Spanish democracy.

Content Summary

Front matter. Title page: Historia de España en el siglo XX / III. La dictadura de Franco; ePUB r1.3 (JeSsE 06.02.14); original title note; Javier Tusell, 2000; digital-editor credits. Publisher’s blurb: “Una obra fundamental para entender la España de hoy.” Series note: four volumes covering a contested century whose knowledge is indispensable for any diagnosis of the present; volume III analyses the stages of Franco’s dictatorship from postwar repression to economic development and the final opening of the regime.

Opening block — El dictador, el régimen y la sociedad

Opens on the night of 1 April 1939: at a quarter past eleven the habitual announcer Fernando Fernández de Córdoba reads the last official war bulletin (no. 982) on the radios of the victorious Spain — “cautivo y desarmado” the Red Army, Franco’s troops have reached their last military objectives — and the less-remembered vivas that accompanied it to the exterior allies of the three preceding years, Portugal, Italy and Germany. The Victory parade of 19 May 1939 (120,000 soldiers; the grand cross of San Fernando imposed by Jordana) and the religious ceremony of 20 May in Santa Bárbara (Franco under palio; sword deposited before the Cristo de Lepanto; Cardinal Gomá) frame the volume’s three subjects: the person of the dictator, the regime as a dictatorship, and the society that lived under it. The ceremonial — military, political and religious mixed as in a medieval warrior society — is presented as evidence of the rupture at the end of the Civil War.

Franco: biografía y praxis política de un dictador

Against the tendency to reduce the regime to a psychological portrait of its founder, Tusell sets Franco’s biography and political practice — the traits that the regime itself made the centre of rumour and expectation, and that the historian must neither inflate into pure personalism nor dissolve into impersonal structure. Born 1892 at El Ferrol in a naval family of six generations; entry in 1907 to the Toledo Academy (youngest cadet of his promotion) after the fleet’s destruction closed the naval path taken by his brother Nicolás; African career, Civil War rise, and the long praxis of rule to 1975.

El franquismo como dictadura

Defines the Spanish regime of 1939–1975 as a dictatorship without making it a Spanish peculiarity: Franco’s conceptions and behaviour expressed a wider European type rather than a unique national path. What was peculiar was that, in Franco’s lifetime, the regime passed from resemblance to one type of dictatorship to another without substantial change of person at the head — a reason to name it by who personified it. Typology against fascist, authoritarian and personal-dictatorship models.

Vencedores y vencidos: Los desastres de la guerra y la represión

From Cambó’s wartime reflection (from emigration, despite conservative leanings and aid to the Franco side) that the “Año triunfal” forgot the torrents of blood, the destruction of wealth and the sediment of rancour, through the disasters of the war and the repression that fell on the defeated after 1 April 1939. Critiques the symbolic “one million dead” figure as exaggerated; engages Salas’s magnitudes (brutal ferocity on both sides, but not exceptional in the contemporary world at roughly over 1 per cent direct war dead).

El exilio y el comienzo de la posguerra en el interior

The exile of 1939 as a rupture in Spanish history — larger, longer and more severed from interior reality than earlier Spanish emigrations. Waves: ~200,000 into France after the fall of the North (35,000 remaining there); ~350,000 crossing after the fall of Catalonia (180,000 combatants); a third wave later. Penury of the French camps; negotiations in which France pressed Franco to take refugees back. Interior postwar for those who remained under the weight of the victors’ order.

La tentación fascista y la supervivencia (1939–1951)

Periodising frame for the first long phase: economic policy makes 1959 a key date, but the decades themselves differ sharply; 1939–51 is the phase of fascist temptation, wartime alignment, cosmetic adaptation after 1945, isolation, and the “noche oscura” of autarky. Predominance of politics over economy; lamentable economic management by those in power, beyond the circumstances of Spain and the world.

El «Nuevo Estado» y la «fascistización» fallida

From the end of the Civil War to the summer of 1940 — when world war seemed decided and Fascist Italy entered — Spain’s internal and institutional drift toward homologation with the Axis (more than in foreign policy alone) and the limits of that “fascistisation.” The months that seemed to decant Spain toward Axis-type institutions.

La tentación intervencionista y la lucha interna (1940–1942)

Against the late-franquista propaganda claim that Germany pressed and Franco resisted, Tusell holds that from the German victory over France there was an absolute identification of Spanish leaders with the Axis cause, prolonged with nuances into 1944; Falangist press treated the defeat of France as the great occasion awaited for decades for a repartition of Europe and French colonial possessions. Internal struggle (Serrano Suñer and others) and the interventionist temptation of 1940–42; Spanish pretensions never had a chance of success because Hitler’s Germany was far from the vision Spanish leaders projected onto it.

Un titubeante camino hacia la neutralidad (1942–1945)

Serrano Suñer’s displacement and Jordana at Foreign Affairs; the hesitant path toward genuine neutrality as the world war turned. Anglo-American landings in North Africa accompanied by assurances that the operation was not directed against Franco, even as Allied distrust remained. Facilities previously enjoyed by Germans and Italians were cut back; Jordana’s December 1942 journey to Portugal. Neutrality favoured by events as much as by any single decision of Franco.

El cambio cosmético: La política del régimen entre 1945 y 1951

At the close of the world war the regime faced simultaneous dangers: uncertainty of internal course, an opposition aiming at rupture or peaceful transition, and external isolation born of its wartime stance. Franco early discovered the need to show an appearance of institutional change through “constitutional” dispositions that barely modified the real power he retained (Ley de Cortes of 1942 read more as wartime than as postwar camouflage). Lequerica’s advice in 1945 that Arrese (and with him the Falange) “debía apartarse de la luz”; from 1944, signs of a will to offer an apparently democratising image.

La oposición de los supervivientes: La izquierda desde 1939 a 1951

The left opposition of the survivors — opened with the figure of Manuel Cortés, the socialist mayor of Mijas who reappeared in April 1969 after thirty years hidden in his house (from age thirty-four to sixty-four) under the amnesty that removed Civil-War responsibilities — from 1939 through guerrilla and exile politics to 1951. The precarious situation of the defeated in 1939, prolonged against expectation after 1945.

El gran momento de la alternativa monárquica

Prieto’s readiness, despite a trajectory close to republicanism, to deal with the monarchists as the path to substituting Franco; Western democratic powers’ preference for a monarchical path of reconciliation. After the Lausanne manifesto of 1945, emissaries between Don Juan and Franco; limited possibilities of agreement — Franco demanding that the Monarchy incarnate “las esencias antiliberales de nuestra Cruzada.”

Franco, aislado

Post-1945 isolation did not begin only with the Allied victory but became severe then; Tusell assigns primary responsibility to Franco’s maintenance of the political system, not only to wartime Axis collaboration. Spain not invited to the San Francisco conference that founded the UN; Mexican delegate’s proposal that future membership exclude the Spanish case. Survival despite the isolation.

La «noche oscura»: Autarquía y racionamiento en la década de los cuarenta

The coherence of early postwar interior and exterior policy; when the European environment changed, the political system did not, adapting only cosmetically. Spain’s postwar condition penurious though destruction was not comparable to Europe after 1945. Autarky and state interventionism before and after 1945, with a social-revolutionary tone often in contradiction with ministers’ dispositions; rationing and the economic “dark night” of the 1940s. Continuity such that 1945 was not a true caesura in economic history.

Cultura y sociedad en la primera posguerra

From the “Edad de Plata” of Spanish letters at the Civil War’s outbreak — fin-de-siècle generation still mature, heirs and new generations productive — to culture and society under the first postwar: rupture, exile of talent (Alberti’s verses on the Atlantic crossing), and the official cultural order.

Los años del consenso: el apogeo del régimen (1951–1965)

The central years as plenitude and apogee: a dictatorship stable enough that passive social acceptance could be called consensus — explained by repression and the dismantling of opposition. By 1951 the worst immediate postwar pressure had been passed.

El final definitivo del aislamiento internacional: El concordato y los pactos con Estados Unidos

Cold War conjuncture of first-order importance for the dictatorship’s survival: with the Korean conflict in 1950 Spain began rapid international rehabilitation culminating in 1953 (Concordat and US pacts). UN path: November 1950 revocation of the 1946 recommendations; entry into FAO (1950), UPU, WHO, ICAO (1951), UNESCO (1952); UN membership mid-December 1955 with a package of nations after Soviet support for the proposition. Concordat with the Holy See (August 1953) — initiative from the Spanish state (Ruiz Giménez as ambassador from 1948) despite advantages to the Church — and US pacts one month later as pure recognition of admission to the international community.

España ante Europa. La descolonización: Marruecos

European democratic memory of the Civil War versus American strategic distance; De Gaulle and Adenauer as examples of conservative European leaders still holding the Spanish dictatorship at institutional distance. Spain before Europe; the decolonisation of Morocco. Regime’s periodic fear of isolation even after rehabilitation.

La política del régimen y la oposición hasta 1956

The 1951 ministerial change among the most complete in the regime’s history — counterweight of tendencies under Franco’s arbitration, yet representing in part the return of the Falange (Girón, Fernández Cuesta, Muñoz Grandes; official reappearance of the Secretariat). Interior politics without major convulsions through the mid-1950s; limited opposition capacity.

Una nueva oposición política

After 1956, a new interior opposition not derived from prewar or exile groups but from among the victors or those hitherto absent from national political life — the germ of what late franquismo called the “oposición moderada.” Opposition and official politics ran contrary paths; only episodically did the first affect the second (most decisively at Munich 1962). Links to cultural evolution.

Por o contra la Falange: La vida política del régimen entre 1956 y 1965

From 1956 the first serious attempt at institutionalisation (Franco allergic to “constitutionalisation,” accepting only the term institutionalisation); blue revival versus technocratic course. Falangists’ sense that Franco was not one of theirs; hatred of radicals toward Carrero; Franco’s instrumental and yet indispensable use of the Falange. Process culminating more clearly after the 1965 cabinet.

El alivio de la autarquía y el cambio en la política económica

Early-1950s growth of national income; end of the ration book in 1951; Spain catching the European postwar “miracles” after exceptional backwardness (low energy consumption per inhabitant). Acceptance of Franco’s Spain as an irremediable evil by the democratic powers, especially the United States, with immediate economic effect in scarce foreign exchange. Road toward the relief of autarky and the 1959 Stabilisation Plan.

De la oposición política a la oposición social

After 1962 (Munich; ministerial change) a new stage in which social protest outweighs strictly political opposition; the “oposición social” consolidated in the second half of the 1960s. Mid-decade, not yet fully irreversible; this section is the prior stage that helps explain it.

La cultura durante el franquismo intermedio

Intermediate-franquismo culture: opening abroad, will to modernise, identification of much of the cultural world with opposition, yet contact with official circles that later fed subversive attitudes. Continuity in culture more marked than the political rupture with the past. The intellectual sector that advanced Spain, for the first time since the Civil War, in a modernising sense; a political class and an intellectual world still present in later Spanish public life.

La cultura popular y el ocio

Cinema, popular arts and leisure as defining marks of the epoch; the porous border between high culture and mass entertainment. 1951 creation of the Ministry of Information and Tourism with a directorate-general for Cinema; new generation of filmmakers and a critical consciousness parallel to literature’s evolution.

Desarrollo económico, apertura y tardofranquismo (1966–1975)

The last decade dominated more than any earlier phase by Franco’s person in decline; zones of indifference left to ministers shrink as succession and crisis concentrate on the aging dictator. Economic growth continuing to modify the closed and authoritarian Spanish society; a political system that seemed to answer to another world wrestling with modernisation’s contradictions, while the European context appeared to impose a course that was not fully followed after initial liberalising measures. Capacity of reaction of those at the head reduced to a minimum compared with the past.

La modernización de la sociedad española

Economic growth paired with deep social transformation of political repercussion. Census 1960–1970: population from 30 to more than 33 million; demographic modernisation (mortality change in the 1950s already Europeanising); internal migrations of the 1960s — roughly four million Spaniards changing residence, drawn to economic resources and fleeing rural stagnation.

El cambio en el catolicismo español

Reception of Vatican II; the Church as both mirror and motor of mental and political change, given its enormous social power. Deep into the 1950s, hierarchy and Catholics still shared identity of Spain, regime and Catholicism (e.g. Aurelio del Pino, bishop of Lérida, on Franco). Nacional-catolicismo as mentality more than precise theology — close bond to the State, “insatiability” that would claim the whole of life — already becoming extravagant as internal Catholic evolution advanced.

La apertura (1965–1969)

“Apertura” as loosening of controls without substantial transformation of the regime; optimism from growth and weak opposition; Press Law and younger politicians of imprecise affiliation, better managers with more bureaucratic formation. Apertura not the same as institutionalisation, but related: Carrero not aligned with the first, yet from 1958 defending the second as prolongation of the regime (“Si el Rey recoge los poderes que tiene V. E. … lo cambiará todo”).

La decisión sucesoria. Matesa y la división interna del régimen

Juan Carlos named successor — López Rodó’s slow process with Carrero (“Operación Salmón”); the last institutionalising decision taken with sufficient unity of the ruling class. 1963 installation of Juan Carlos at Zarzuela; Franco’s alternative thoughts (renewable regency; Alfonso de Borbón); demand for “identificación absoluta.” Fraga’s November 1965 foreign-press remarks; Matesa scandal and internal division.

La protesta laboral. El terrorismo

Rising opposition in the final phase: labour protest and terrorism; the regime coexisted with both; historical weight lies as much in that coexistence as in the pressure itself. First half of the 1950s as minimum of opposition activity; final phase the opposite. New climate influencing the opposition toward the path of unity; coincidence of journalists’ and lawyers’ movements with social protest.

El tardofranquismo: Carrero Blanco, presidente

Franco past seventy-five; failure of the old arbitral role among the victorious families; foreign visitors’ portraits of an old man who scarcely spoke. Tardofranquismo defined not only by physical decline but by lack of clear course, public-order problems, fragmentation of the political class, proliferation of antagonistic leaderships, and paralysis before the most urgent problems. October 1969 government with Carrero as vice-president, labelled monocolor by adversaries (López Rodó contesting the label in his memoirs).

El tardofranquismo: el Gobierno Arias Navarro

Almost the only continuity between Carrero’s government and Arias Navarro’s was Franco’s irremediable physical decline — senile, inarticulate, short steps, tearful, “escuchaba, pero no oía” (Fraga). Appointment of Arias as testimony to the definitive rise of the family circle in regime politics (Nieto Antúnez, Arias; Carrero never had much standing there). Fernández Miranda marginalised despite acting to check Iniesta’s excessive Guardia Civil instructions. Juan Carlos not consulted in the cabinet’s gestation — raising the question how far the change put the succession itself in doubt.

Actividad de la oposición: el camino hacia la unidad

Social protest rising slowly to 1967, sharply from 1973, peaking in the months after Franco’s death; not by itself able to overthrow the regime, but the backdrop of late politics. Terrorism incapable of substituting the regime, yet constant proof of repressive impotence and a mobiliser of part of Basque society; by this stage ETA’s brutality had alienated the relative benevolence with which part of the opposition had viewed it into the early 1970s. Path toward opposition unity.

España y el mundo occidental

External context of the final phase; the Saharan/Moroccan crisis in the regime’s last weeks. Castiella at Foreign Affairs 1957–1969, the longest-serving foreign minister of the franquismo — nationalist, youthful Falange attraction, promoted by the Catholic family of the regime (Vatican embassy then the Ministry); programme combining prior lines (Arab world) with new ones. Spain’s peculiar place in Western Europe and Western defence.

La descolonización tardía: Guinea y el Sahara

Carrero against decolonisation, Castiella promoting it (partly with Gibraltar in view); Sahara and Guinea declared provinces 1958–59, then the retreat — Guinea first, Sahara in the terminal crisis. Difference from Salazar’s Portugal: colonies meant more to Portugal, and Salazar exercised more direct foreign-policy responsibility than Franco at this stage.

¿Una cultura comprometida?

Late-franquismo culture: less simple increase of cultural belligerence than development of culture and society beside the political system, with periodic conflict. Thought and social sciences on premises wholly alien to the official political world; literature shedding exaggerated social-political pretension for experimentalism not directly tied to political or ethical demonstration. Theatre and cinema, more exposed to censorship, retained significant cases of belligerence. 1969 as a cultural as well as political marker. Homologation and recovery as keywords; disappearance of the interminable debate on national peculiarity.

España a la muerte de Franco

Last year of Franco’s life — pathos and esperpento; July 1974 thrombophlebitis from hard gift shoes; re-learning to speak and walk by narrating the Moroccan campaigns; obstinacy in remaining in power until death in November 1975. Daily routine of receiving dozens of people while barely articulating a word; sedentary distractions (television); impressibility of age, Parkinson’s, and the impact of Saharan/Moroccan news on a man whose life had centred on the Moroccan wars. Sociologically the regime represented less and less of society and was increasingly available for other options. Younger regime politicians seeking an exit (Tácito; the independent parliamentary group of summer 1975 with future UCD figures and names such as Primo de Rivera). Society not strongly politicised against the regime in Martín Villa’s sense of “motivaciones apremiantes,” yet desiring a democratic order in principle: surveys show democratic preference rising (1966: 35 per cent for decisions by persons elected by the people vs a leading man; 1974: 60 per cent); conformity values (peace, order, tradition) from 71 per cent (1966) to 55 per cent (1975); disconformity values (justice, liberty, democracy) from 20 to 33 per cent; belief that parties would be “beneficial” from 12 per cent (1971) to 56 per cent (spring 1975). At Franco’s death, ~72 per cent wanted the King to grant freedom of expression and ~70 per cent universal suffrage. The non-traumatic transition’s starting point lay in this panorama and in the political capacity of those who led it.

Bibliografía

Selective bibliography with asterisks for especially original recent monographs. Opens with Stanley Payne, El régimen de Franco, 1936–1975 (Alianza, 1987) as the best overall study, and the contrasting Biescas–Tuñón de Lara antifranquista synthesis (España bajo la dictadura franquista, Labor, 1980) versus Ricardo de la Cierva’s Historia del franquismo (Planeta, 1975 and 1978), closer to the regime. Continues through specialised works on politics, economy, Church, opposition, culture and foreign relations (Fontana ed. España bajo el franquismo, Crítica, 1986; congresses and journal special numbers from the early 1990s onward). The digital edition closes with a short author biography of Tusell (Barcelona 26 August 1945 – 8 February 2005): formation in philosophy, history and political science; teaching from 1966; production on elections and parties, Alfonso XIII, caciquismo, Primo de Rivera’s coup, Christian democracy, Franco and the regime, Spain and the Second World War, Franco and the Count of Barcelona, Catholics under franquismo, the democratic opposition, Carrero, Arias Navarro, and Juan Carlos — all ordered to the obsession of his generation with democracy in Spain.

Relationship to the Wiki

Tusell III gives this wiki its continuous scholarly history of Franco’s Spain across the whole span from the Civil War’s end to the dictator’s death. The early chapters (1939–45 fascistisation, interventionist temptation, hesitant neutrality) overlap the Rest of Europe 1919–45 wiki, where the same volume is also held; the middle and late chapters (isolation and autarky, Concordat and US pacts, developmentalism, apertura, succession, Carrero, Arias Navarro, opposition unity, late decolonisation, Spain at Franco’s death) fall squarely inside this folder’s 1946–present span. Beside the de Gaulle Discours et Messages tomes (T2–T5), which supply the French case in the General’s own voice from the Fourth Republic through the close of the Fifth Republic presidency, Tusell supplies the Spanish case as modern secondary synthesis. Volume IV of the same series (La transición democrática y el gobierno socialista) continues from this endpoint into the Transition and the Socialist governments and is processed as a sibling source in this wiki.
