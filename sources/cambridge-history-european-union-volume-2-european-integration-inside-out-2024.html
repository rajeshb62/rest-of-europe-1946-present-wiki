NoteThe second volume of the Cambridge History of the European Union, examining the EU from an “inside-out” perspective — the internal developments that shaped the integration process. Unlike a strict chronological narrative, the volume is organised thematically: Part I traces the principles defining integration through the treaties and their changes over time (with Brexit a core milestone); Part II considers the instruments within the architecture of integration, with special focus on policies, money/the euro and enlargement; Part III concentrates on the narratives surrounding integration — the concepts, goals and ideas (the “longue durée”, peace, European culture, religion and its absence, prosperity, solidarity and democracy) that did and did not speak to the hearts and minds of Europeans. The editors stress that every chapter builds on original multinational and/or transnational archival research, stretching beyond “silo studies” toward a richer, more comprehensive, interdisciplinary picture

Content Summary

Reflections on the History and Historiography of European Integration (Mathieu Segers & Steven Van Hecke). The editorial introduction. The editors survey the shifting scholarly effort to explain “why and how” of European integration — the “big questions” of ideas, interests and institutions — and argue that new disciplinary perspectives (beyond the usual suspects of history, political science, law and economics) are needed to rejuvenate the research in light of the transformations since “Maastricht”. They stress the continuing topicality of the classic questions: the position of reunited Germany in post-Cold War Europe (“the German Question”, renewed since the euro, migration and Covid-19 crises and the Russian war in Ukraine); the British relationship with the continent culminating in Brexit; and the geopolitical role of the EU vis-à-vis revisionist powers like Russia and China. The CHEU is presented as a diachronic, multidisciplinary and multi-actor reconceptualisation of integration history, its chapters based on original multinational and transnational archival research, and it revisits the big questions — why did Europeans integrate, and how does the resulting system work, with what outcomes.

Part I — Milestones: Treaties and Treaty Changes

Chapter 1: Early Forms of European Unity (Gérard Bossuat, Professor Emeritus, University of Cergy-Pontoise; Jean Monnet ad Personam Chair, History of European Unity). The pre-1945 and wartime origins of the idea and practice of European unity — the long genealogy of schemes for a united Europe before the Communities.

Chapter 2: From Messina and Rome to the Single European Act (Michael Gehler, Professor of History, University of Hildesheim). The treaty history from the Messina conference (1955) and the Treaties of Rome (1957) establishing the EEC and Euratom, through the consolidation of the 1960s and the crisis decades, to the Single European Act (1986) — the legal-constitutional milestones of the Community’s first three decades.

Chapter 3: The Making of the European Union (Sophie Vanhoonacker, Professor of European Governance, Maastricht University). The negotiation of the Treaty of Maastricht on European Union (1991) — the founding act of the EU, with the three pillars, EMU, and the new competences. (Contains Tables 3.1 and 3.2, key dates for the SEA and the Maastricht Treaty.)

Chapter 4: From Maastricht and Copenhagen to Amsterdam and Nice (Desmond Dinan, Professor of Public Policy, George Mason University). The treaty revisions of the 1990s — the accession process launched at Copenhagen (1993), the Amsterdam Treaty (1997) and the Nice Treaty (2000).

Chapter 5: The Constitution Project, Lisbon and Beyond (Jörg Monar, Professor, Vrije Universiteit Brussel / College of Europe). The failure of the Constitutional Treaty (2004–05 referenda) and the Lisbon Treaty (2007, in force 2009), and the further evolution of the Union’s constitutional order thereafter.

Chapter 6: Moving beyond British Exceptionalism (N. Piers Ludlow, Professor of International History, London School of Economics). Britain’s troubled relationship with European integration — from the EFTA years and the failed membership applications to membership and the eventual vote for Brexit — read against the grain of “British exceptionalism”.

Part II — Instruments of Integration

Money and Society. Chapter 7: In the Name of Social Stability: The European Payments Union (Jorrit Steehouder, historian). The European Payments Union (EPU, 1950–58), the postwar intra-European clearing and payments mechanism that laid the monetary foundations for European economic cooperation. Chapter 8: Competition versus Planning: A Battle That Shaped European Integration (Laurent Warlouzet, Professor of History, Sorbonne University). The contest between competition-policy liberalism and economic planning in shaping the European economic order. Chapter 9: Commercial Banks, the Eurodollar Market and the Beginnings of Monetary Integration (Carlo Edoardo Altamura, Swiss National Science Foundation Ambizione Fellow, Graduate Institute, Geneva). The role of private commercial banks and the Eurodollar market in the early monetary integration of the 1960s. Chapter 10: From the Werner Report to the Start of EMU (Hubert Zimmermann, Professor of International Relations, Philipps University Marburg). The monetary odyssey from the Werner Report (1970), through the “snake” and the EMS, to the start of Economic and Monetary Union. (Contains Figures 10.1–10.3: inflation rates 1969–80, EMS realignment rounds 1979–95, convergence of European interest rates prior to EMU.) Chapter 11: The Euro Area Crisis: From Pre-history to Aftermath (Amy Verdun, Professor of Political Science, University of Victoria). The euro-area crisis of the 2010s and its aftermath.

Challenges of Expansion: Protection and Security. Chapter 12: The Institutional and Legal Culture of European Integration (Philip Bajon, Max Planck Institute for Legal History and Legal Theory, Frankfurt). The distinctive institutional and legal culture of the EU. Chapter 13: The Formation of the Migration Regime of the EU (Emmanuel Comte, Senior Research Fellow, ELIAMEP; Vienna School of International Studies). The construction of the EU’s migration and asylum regime. Chapter 14: The Constitutional Dimension: Centralisation, Democratisation and the Rule of Law (András Jakab, University of Salzburg, and Lando Kirchmair, Bundeswehr University Munich). The constitutional development of the Union — centralisation, democratisation and the rule of law. Chapter 15: EU Enlargement: Origins and Practice (Marc Maresceau, Professor Emeritus, Ghent University). The enlargement of the Union from the Six to the Twenty-eight — its origins and practice.

Part III — Narratives and Outcomes

War and Peace. Chapter 16: A Global Perspective on European Cooperation and Integration since 1918 (Anne-Isabelle Richard, University of Leiden). European cooperation and integration in global perspective since the First World War. (Contains Figure 16.1: Coudenhove-Kalergi’s Paneuropa world map, 1923.) Chapter 17: War, Peace and Memory: Franco-German Reconciliation (Carine Germond, Nanyang Technological University). The narrative of war, peace and memory in the Franco-German reconciliation — the decline of the “hereditary enemies” narrative and the rise of the “couple franco-allemand” / “deutsch-französische Versöhnung” / “Franco-German engine” narratives. (Contains Figures 17.1–17.8: the quantitative history of these narratives in French-, German- and English-language literatures, and the Merkozy and Merkron media narratives 2008–22 and 2015–21.) Chapter 18: The ‘Saints’ of European Integration: From Visionaries to Architects (Michael Gehler). The founding figures of integration — from the “visionaries” (Monnet, Schuman, Adenauer, de Gasperi) to the “architects” of later decades.

Prosperity and Solidarity. Chapter 19: The EU and the Narrative of Prosperity (Claudia Hiepel, University of Duisburg-Essen). The EU’s self-narrative of prosperity and its limits. Chapter 20: Changing Europe’s Economic History (Ivan T. Berend, Distinguished Research Professor, UCLA). Europe’s economic history and its transformation — the divergence of central/eastern from northwestern Europe and the postwar convergence. (Contains Tables 20.1–20.5: per capita GDP 1870–1938 and postwar, Central Europe as a percentage of northwestern Europe, regional growth 1913–2013, and world HDI in 2016.) Chapter 21: The EU and the Narrative of Solidarity (Malcolm Ross, University of Sussex). The EU’s narrative of solidarity and its contested reality. Chapter 22: European Solidarity: The Difficult Art of Managing Interdependence (Amandine Crespy, Professor, Vrije Universiteit Brussel, and Nicolas Verschueren). The practical management of interdependence as the “difficult art” of European solidarity.

Democracy and Legitimacy. Chapter 23: Ideologies of EU Democracy since 1950 (Claudia Sternberg, University College London). The ideologies of EU democracy from the 1950s. Chapter 24: Democratic Challenges since Maastricht (Wouter Wolfs, University of Leuven). The democratic challenges of the Union since Maastricht — the persistent question of the EU’s democratic legitimacy.
