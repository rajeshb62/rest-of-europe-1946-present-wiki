AuthorIlko-Sascha Kowalczuk — German historian (born 1967 in East Berlin); among the leading historians of the GDR; author of the standard work Endspiel. Die Revolution von 1989 in der DDR (C.H. Beck, 2009, several editions) and numerous further books on GDR history
EditionVerlag C.H. Beck, München, first published August 2019; ISBN 978-3-406-74020-6 (Print) / 978-3-406-74021-3 (E-Book); published for the thirtieth anniversary of the fall of the Wall
StructurePersonal opening (Zwischen Aufbruch und Abbruch) + essay proper + twenty-two topical chapters + closing chapter + Danksagung, Anmerkungen, Literaturhinweise, Personenregister; extracted plain text of 8,033 lines
Period1989/90 to the present — the transformation of East Germany from the fall of the Wall through reunification and the three decades since, and how that past shapes the eastern German present
NoteAn avowed “essay” (in the literal sense of a Versuch, an attempt) written to mark the thirtieth anniversary of 1989: a pointed, opinionated reflection on how the GDR was “taken over” by West Germany and what the social, economic, political and cultural consequences of that takeover have been for eastern Germany. Kowalczuk, himself an East German who served in the 1990s as a member of the Enquete-Kommission of the German Bundestag on the SED dictatorship, sets out to explain “how our present has become historical” — placing hard facts beside personal experience, and pushing deliberately provocative theses about the successes and failures of the transformation (the German subtitle of the book’s publisher advertising calls it “the political book of the hour”). It is as much a critical history of the transformation as a contribution to contemporary debate about democracy, populism and extremism in eastern Germany

Content Summary

Zwischen Aufbruch und Abbruch (opening). Kowalczuk opens with intimate personal and family vignettes that frame the whole essay. The chapter introduces the theme of East German hopes and their frustration through individual stories: “Dieter,” his wife’s father, an East German building-firm director who greeted the fall of the Wall with renewed energy but saw his business collapse under the burden of “Altschulden” (inherited debts) and the privatisation regime of the Treuhand, ending bankrupt, privately liable and dying of cancer on the day he was summoned to court; and Kowalczuk’s own father, an SED member from conviction, a financial economist, born into an ethnically-German family expelled from Bohemia in 1946. Through these histories Kowalczuk sets up his central theme: the coexistence in East German biographies of Anpassung and Selbstbehauptung (adaptation and self-assertion), of Mitmachen and Widerspruch (complicity and dissent), of Überzeugung and Verrat (conviction and betrayal) — often within a single person.

Der Essay. The framing introduction. Kowalczuk explains that he writes to show “how our present has become historical,” drawing on three decades of scholarly work on the history and consequences of communism in Germany and Europe. He situates the essay in a moment when Western conceptions of democracy and freedom have come under pressure from within as well as without. He insists the essay is not about the infinite details of history but about the great lines and questions that impose themselves if we want to understand the present — hence “a look back.” He warns about the limits of statistics and opinion polls (which “sharpen and invent realities”), and announces that he will deliberately sharpen and provoke in order to make problems visible.

Der letzte Republikgeburtstag. The last GDR republic anniversary — the fortieth anniversary of the founding of the GDR on 7 October 1989 — as the moment when the regime’s terminal crisis became unmistakable. Kowalczuk describes the atmosphere in East Berlin on that day, contrasting the regime’s anniversary celebrations with the reality of a collapsing state; Chilean emigrants in the GDR are quoted comparing the tension to the eve of the Chilean coup.

Unmittelbare Folgen des Mauerfalls. The immediate consequences of the fall of the Wall on 9 November 1989: the unprecedented mass movement of people, the opening of the border, and the sudden, disorienting entry of West German reality into the East.

Vom Runden Tisch zu freien Wahlen. From the Central Round Table to free elections. Kowalczuk traces the negotiated transition of power in the GDR — the emergence of the “Zentraler Runder Tisch” from December 1989, where government and opposition sat together, the dissolution of the SED’s monopoly, and the road to the first free Volkskammer election of 18 March 1990.

Neue Erfahrung: Arbeitslosigkeit. The new experience of unemployment. The collapse of the GDR economy threw a third of East German employment onto the streets: the GDR had 9.7 million employed in 1989 but only 6.2 million by the end of 1993. Kowalczuk analyses the labour-market catastrophe, the role of the Treuhand in closing plants, the DGB’s failings in the East, and the Reichsbahn strike as a signal of the new conflicts.

Der Beitritt: alternativlos? “The accession: was there no alternative?” A critical examination of the process by which the GDR acceded to the Federal Republic under Article 23 rather than a constitution-drafting path under Article 146 of the Basic Law. Kowalczuk recounts the cool reception of the Modrow government in Bonn in February 1990, the “deutsch-deutsche Wahlkampf” of Kohl’s election campaign, and the argument that the accession route was presented as “alternativlos” (without alternative) — a claim he interrogates.

Was wäre wenn …? “What if …?” A counterfactual reflection on whether the transformation could have taken a different, less one-sided form — what a genuinely negotiated unity, or a longer transition, might have looked like.

In was für einer Verfassung? “In what kind of constitution?” The constitutional question of German unity: the failure to give the new Germany a new constitution and the adoption instead of the Basic Law, and what that choice meant for eastern Germans.

Wer sind Ostdeutsche? “Who are the East Germans?” A chapter on the very definition of “East German” — the historical space that no longer exists since 1945, the five new Länder (Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Brandenburg, Sachsen-Anhalt, Thüringen, Sachsen) of about 12.6 million inhabitants on roughly 108,000 square kilometres, and the special case of Berlin with its 3.6 million inhabitants.

Ostdeutsche als «Andere». East Germans as “the others.” Kowalczuk analyses how East Germans were constructed as a distinct, exotic, often deficient “other” in the unified republic, and the asymmetry of mutual perception between East and West.

Die Forderung: «Anpassung» and Die Angepassten. The demand for “adaptation” and “the adapted.” On the one-sided expectation that East Germans adapt to Western norms, institutions and habits after 1990, and the figure of the “Angepasste” — the accommodating East German.

Die Hauptstadtdebatte. The Berlin capital debate — the 1991 Bundestag decision to make Berlin (not Bonn) the seat of government and parliament, and what it signified.

Stagnation. Economic stagnation and only a slight recovery, driven above all by the booming construction sector in the East.

Die Treuhand. The Treuhandanstalt — the most important shaper of the first half of the 1990s. Kowalczuk analyses how the Treuhand privatised, liquidated or “gesundgeschrumpft” (shrunk to health) some 8,000 Volkseigene Betriebe (people’s enterprises), and the social devastation this caused.

Die Eigentumsfrage. The property question: the restitution of property expropriated under the GDR and its enormous administrative and social costs (“Rückgabe vor Entschädigung”).

Die Landwirtschaft. Agriculture: the GDR’s collectivised agriculture (large LPG cooperatives) confronted with Western agricultural policy, and the collapse of East German farming.

Der ökonomische Kollaps als Kulturkampf. The economic collapse as a cultural struggle — Kowalczuk’s argument that the economic transformation was experienced and fought out by East Germans also as an assault on their ways of life and self-respect.

Das Unverständnis für Ostdeutschland. The West’s failure to understand East Germany.

Das Beschäftigungsproblem. The employment problem in the East across the decades since 1990.

Die kulturelle Ausgrenzung. Cultural exclusion — the marginalisation of East German voices, experiences and cultural producers in the unified republic.

Mobilität als Problem. Mobility as a problem — the permanent East-West migration of the young and skilled and its demographic consequences.

Rentenprobleme. Pension problems — the long-running and politically explosive grievance over East German pension entitlements.

Gewinnerinnen oder Verliererinnen? Winners or losers? Kowalczuk weighs who profited and who lost from the transformation, insisting the answers are not as simple as the clichés suggest.

Neue Benachteiligungen. New disadvantages — the forms of structural disadvantage East Germans continued to face.

Der Elitenaustausch an den Universitäten. The elite exchange at the universities — how the East German academic and intellectual elite was replaced after 1990, and the consequences of that break.

Ost-Eliten im vereinigten Deutschland. East German elites in united Germany — the small number of East Germans in leading positions of the economy, politics, media and culture, and the resulting “Kolonialisierung” debates.

Irgendwie alle! “Somehow everyone!” On the East-West institutional fusions and their bitter, often farcical conflicts — the “war of the arts” over the fusion of the East and West Berlin Academies of the Arts, where Heiner Müller, last president of the East Academy, warned in July 1990 that what was happening was “a process of conquest, of subjugation, not of unification,” invoking Cortés and the Catholic churches built on the Aztec temples of Mexico.

Gesellschaftspolitische Dimensionen der Aufarbeitung. The socio-political dimensions of coming to terms with the past — the Stasi files, the Gauck authority, and the social conflicts of “Aufarbeitung” (the official reckoning with the SED dictatorship).

Demokratie ohne Demokraten. “Democracy without democrats.” One of the essay’s central chapters: Kowalczuk notes that 87.5 per cent of the GDR population in 1989 had no personal experience of a functioning democratic state (only the generation born 1923 or earlier had known Weimar), that the civic democracy had been systematically vilified under both the Nazi dictatorship and the GDR, and that this legacy made eastern Germany a “democracy without democrats” — a society that inherited democratic institutions without democratic habit.

Antisemitismus, Illiberale Einstellungen, Nationalismus, Rassismus. Four successive chapters on eastern German attitudes: antisemitism, illiberal attitudes, nationalism and racism — measured against polling data and the rise of right-wing populism.

Medien in Ostdeutschland. The media landscape in eastern Germany — the takeover of the East German media by Western companies, the collapse of the GDR’s newspapers, and the distinctive eastern media market.

Wahlen im Osten. Elections in the East: persistently lower turnout than the federal average since the first all-German election of 2 December 1990 (swinging between 82.2 per cent in 1998 and 70.8 per cent in 2009), the East-West split in Bundestag results, and the success of right-wing populists and extremists in the East.

Zwischen 1989: Hoffnungslosigkeit und 1990: Hoffnungen. The closing chapter — “Between 1989: hopelessness and 1990: hopes.” Kowalczuk contrasts the East Germans’ hopelessness as they entered 1989 (when even the hope of change was rare and a larger minority fled, risking their lives) with the boundless hope with which they entered 1990 and reunification. He catalogues the groups that were later disappointed — the victims of the communists (whom the Rechtsstaat did not properly honour or compensate), the loyal SED system-goers (who felt disadvantaged and humiliated), the workers who lost their jobs, the scientists whose knowledge was no longer needed, the farmers whose agriculture got by without them, the civil-rights activists whose counsel the citizens no longer valued. He ends by sketching “Zukunftsvisionen für Ostdeutschland” (future visions for East Germany) in the early 1990s, and reflects on the fragmenting, globalising world and the temptation of new totalitarian claims.
