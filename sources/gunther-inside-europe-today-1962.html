AuthorJohn Gunther (1901–1970) — American journalist and popular historian; correspondent and chronicler of the Inside series. Seventh Inside book after Inside Europe (1936), Inside Asia (1939/1942), Inside Latin America (1941), Inside U.S.A. (1947/1952), Inside Africa (1955), and Inside Russia Today (1958)
EditionHarper & Brothers, New York, Revised Edition 1962 (first pub. July 1961). Completely revised and reset; Author’s Note claims 790 textual changes and 12,400+ words of new material, above all to cover the Berlin Wall (13 August 1961) and other events of late 1961

Useful for Adenauer/FRG and Berlin 1961, de Gaulle and Algeria, the Macmillan years and Britain’s Common Market dilemma, NATO stresses, early EEC vs EFTA, and Western readings of Khrushchev and the satellites

Content Summary

Cold-war Europe survey framed as a fresh look a quarter-century after Inside Europe. Gunther travels personalities and regimes from Bonn and Berlin through Paris, the Low Countries, Iberia, Italy and the Mediterranean, Austria, Britain, the Atlantic alliance and the two European trading blocs, Scandinavia, and finally Khrushchev and the Soviet sphere — closing on whether general war remains unlikely and whether Western Europe’s new economic unity can harden into a “Great Power.”

Author’s Note

First published July 1961; this revised edition appears less than a year later — completely revised, reset, and republished. Events added or expanded include Soviet orbital flights, Khrushchev’s resumption of nuclear testing, the Portuguese Angola revolt, Congo deterioration, the France–Algeria deadlock, English political developments, the German general election of September 1961, British negotiations with the Common Market, elections in Greece, Turkey, Norway, and Belgium, and above all the building of the wall between East and West Berlin on 13 August 1961. Stresses that Inside Europe Today is a totally different book from 1936 Inside Europe (no textual resemblance). Thanks critics and correspondents; counts 790 textual changes and 12,400+ words of new material. Positions the book as the seventh Inside title.

1. By Way of Introduction

Sets the comparative frame: twenty-six years after Inside Europe, when Hitler, Mussolini, and Stalin dominated a continent heading for war. Lists the obvious disappearances (the three despots; smaller dictators; the Third Reich; Italian monarchy; Third and Fourth French Republics) and the new vocabulary (“Iron Curtain,” cold war, peaceful coexistence, Hiroshima). Central contrast with 1936: a general war in the near future is unlikely — Khrushchev has no present intention of making one, whereas Hitler was bound to. Catalogue of structural changes: two Germanys; satellite belt from Baltic to Black Sea (~115 million people under Soviet dominion); Tito’s Yugoslavia and Albanian special cases; free neutralized Austria (1955 withdrawal / Geneva Summit entry ticket); democracies restored in western Germany, Italy, Greece; only Franco, Salazar, and (intermittently) de Valera carrying over from the 1930s; Franco–German reconciliation as the most important postwar political event; genocide and the shrinkage of European Jewry; League dead, UN alive (tribute to Hammarskjöld). Previews themes — Common Market, disarmament, and the two-power world — before the country chapters begin.

2. The Old Man on the Rhine

Portrait of Konrad Adenauer (Der Alte), seen early 1960 at eighty-four looking fifty-five: Rhoendorf villa, boccie, Rhine wine, Schaumburg Palace routine. Career that effectively began at seventy-two; physical stamina and sharp manner; shaken but not finished by 1961 events. Character sketch — Catholic Rhinelander, anti-Prussian, anti-Nazi past as Cologne mayor, long exile from politics under Hitler. Little taste for abstract economics yet an enthusiastic Common Market advocate who overrules Erhard on tariffs because EEC serves his dearest wish: political integration of Germany, France, Italy, and Benelux (Hallstein at Brussels; “United States of Europe,” really western Europe). Fixed, implacable enmity to the Soviet Union (“John Foster Adenauer”); contrast with de Gaulle and Macmillan, who still pursue détente after the failed May 1960 Summit. Assessment of twelve years’ achievement: bridge from Nazi ruin to parliamentary democracy; Franco–German rapprochement; West Germany’s entry into the civilized community — and the succession anxiety that hangs over Bonn whenever Der Alte is mentioned.

3. Pressures in the Federal Republic

Asks whether Germany has really changed after territorial shrinkage, partition, and the disappearance of Prussia. Gunther’s fieldwork questions: authority and the new democracy; attitudes to Hitler; Nazi revival risk; nationalism; another war? Surveys the Wirtschaftswunder and its makers — especially Ludwig Erhard (free-market “socially minded” economy; American jeep that drafted him into Bavarian government in 1945) and the succession contest (Erhard vs Etzel, Lemmer; Free Democrat coalition fluidity after 1961). Causes of the miracle: industriousness; new plant after bombing and dismantling; Marshall Plan (~$3 billion); refugee/expellee labour. Pressures: expellees and eastern territories; rearmament and NATO; Social Democratic opposition; the Berlin crisis and the wall’s shock to unification hopes. Returns to the lesson question — whether catastrophe has tamed German aggressiveness.

4. Berlin—Outpost and Pivot

Arrival by Pan Am corridor over Communist territory — contrast with sitting on coal in a stripped DC-4 during the 1948 airlift. West Berlin’s Wirtschaftswunder climax (Kurfürstendamm shops vs 1948 cigarette economy) beside still-visible ruin (Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church as “unfilled molar”; citizens refuse full restoration as a war memorial). City as outpost and pivot of the cold war; Allied access; morale. Core of the 1962 revision: night of 13 August 1961 — East German regime builds the wall (~26½ miles), plugging the refugee escape hatch; Gunther’s verdict that it gave the Communists a substantial if temporary victory (status-quo violation without effective Western riposte; West Berlin risk of withering; mortal blow to unification hopes). Refugee arithmetic: ~3.35 million from the Soviet Zone 1945–1961; Marienfelde screening; airlift-out by Air France, BEA, Pan Am (Lufthansa barred). Russian tanks in East Berlin (26 October) as foretaste. Berlin as miniature of a Europe cut in half.

5. Soviet Sector, Soviet Zone

Drive through Brandenburger Tor onto Unter den Linden before the wall: spiritual deadness, thinner traffic, greater destruction left as didactic rubble. East Germany celebrating 8 May (unconditional surrender) as national holiday — a Russian subtlety. Portrait of the German Democratic Republic under Walter Ulbricht: real country, thoroughly subjugated; Red Army presence; economic and police controls; contrast of living standards with the West. Collectivization of agriculture (1960) as a driver of the refugee flood. Speculation on neutralization “on the Austrian model” only at a hypothetical level. Zone as the other half of the German question that Berlin crystallizes.

6. The Person of de Gaulle

Reverses the 1936 formula: then Germany was one person (Hitler) and France many; now France is one person — Charles de Gaulle — while Germany, though Adenauer-dominated, still has plural consequential figures. No de Gaulle in Inside Europe; then an inconspicuous colonel; within four years Free French leader. Personal sketches: rudeness and Olympian lunar grandeur; Rue Solférino “Moi!” anecdote (pro-Americans vs himself); physical presence at seventy-one. Wartime and Fourth Republic exile; return in 1958 and the Fifth Republic constitution that concentrates power in the presidency. Pride, independence from Anglo-American direction, and the claim to speak for France alone.

7. Changes and Perplexities in France

Two major phenomena: the man (de Gaulle) and the country-problem (Algeria). Yet nonpolitical France feels more relaxed — boom, fading 1940 humiliation, stability under the General even as army disaffection and Algeria erode him. Economic sketch: GNP ~63billion(1958), 4.59 billion by end-1957. Homogeneous, balanced France vs still-fragmented Italy. Political map under the Fifth Republic; centre coalitions; Communist and socialist forces; European policy and the German rapprochement; cultural and social notes beneath the constitutional experiment.

8. The Army, Algeria, and Africa

Only a critical worsening in Algeria could upset the regime. January 1960 and April 1961 ultras/army revolts handled with dexterity — but Salan, Lagaillarde, and last-ditch colons remain threats; fear that if de Gaulle dies with Algeria unsolved the army might seize power (possibly even a “mixed” or leftist dictatorship, given Indo-China veterans steeped in Maoist revolutionary war doctrine). Ambiguous, divided army replacing the old Saint-Cyr Catholic caste. Deadlock with the FLN; negotiation hopes and settler resistance. Broader African retreat — Afrique Noire, Community structures, and the wound of imperial liquidation as context for metropolitan politics.

9. The Benelux Constellation

Belgium: dense, rich, industrious — yet riven by Fleming/Walloon cleavage (language, religion, jobs); “royal question” near–civil war (1950); winter 1960–61 austerity-strike crisis; successive short governments. Congo independence as tragic mismanagement — right to be free conceded, preparation denied. Netherlands: two queens (Juliana; Wilhelmina as Princess); sea, reclaiming land, commercial pragmatism; New Guinea / Indonesia residual imperial flashpoint; intricate confessional party system; Foreign Minister Luns as foreign-policy voice beside Premier de Quay. Luxembourg: tiny but original NATO member; Joseph Bech as distinguished long-serving statesman. Benelux as both a working customs/political experiment and a microcosm of Western Europe’s language and colonial aftershocks.

10. Iberian Peninsula

Portugal under Salazar: Estado Novo / União Nacional; corporative chambers; Rerum Novarum / Quadragesimo Anno social doctrine; “authoritarian” rather than “totalitarian” self-image — mild procedures, stifled opposition, secret police. Delgado presidential challenge (1958); Santa Maria seizure by Galvão (January 1961); Angola revolt and UN pressure. Spain: “five-minute glimpse” dominated by the Civil War’s sterilizing legacy and Franco’s unbroken rule since 1939; Axis wartime leanings still rankle; UN member (1955) but kept out of NATO by northern democratic governments despite US willingness; moral boycott after V-E Day; continued denial of civil liberty. Closing caution: dangerous for a democracy (the United States) to become too closely involved with a dictator or semi-dictator.

11. What’s Going On in Italy

Arithmetic of coalition: Christian Democrats (~273 of 596) short of a majority; church party with right, centre, and left wings; Vatican weight. Left: Communists (~140) and Nenni Socialists (~88); permanent chord is the attempt to peel Nenni from the Communists (apertura) — favoured by President Gronchi, opposed by the Vatican, unattractive to Nenni himself. Sketch of Gronchi (ex–Partito Popolare; undersecretary briefly under Mussolini then twenty years out; man of corridors). Economic “miracle” and persistent poverty; south and reform; Fanfani and DC manoeuvre; NATO membership as left–right fault line; contrast with Mussolini’s balcony Italy — Palazzo Venezia now an exhibition hall.

12. Along the Mediterranean

Greece: overcrowded kingdom under Paul and Frederika; Karamanlis vs splintered opposition and fellow-travelers; NATO vs neutralist tug; Balkan pact strains with Turkey over Cyprus; American aid and heavy defence burden. Cyprus: “ulcer removed” — independence 16 August 1960; Makarios; EOKA legacy; Greco–Turkish–British settlement ending partition demand; AKEL; still-tender NATO southeastern flank. Turkey: Atatürk’s secular republic under strain; Menderes deposed (1960) and hanged; army’s tutelary role; NATO/Baghdad Pact geopolitics; U-2 staging; limit to even a valued ally’s credit. Closing Mediterranean notes on dictatorship involvement and the people who eventually kick regimes out.

13. Austria Felix

Opens with Gunther’s older magazine hymn to Vienna (water, beer, coffee houses) — still largely true — then three profound postwar changes: (1) Vienna’s atmosphere — charms intact but no longer imperial, a fascinating minor provincial capital (population down from ~2.2 million to 1.6); Jewish cultural world gutted; (2) neutrality after the 1955 State Treaty and four-power withdrawal (neutrality pledged by Austrian law more than by treaty text; Outer Seven / GATT, not EEC or NATO; air-space and overflights sensitivities); (3) the enduring OeVP–Socialist coalition that yokes clericals and socialists who once fought (Dollfuss, February 1934). Leadership notes (Raab, Pittermann, Kreisky); FPÖ and tiny Communists; US aid memory ($1.5 billion). South Tyrol / Alto Adige quarrel with Italy. Coda: Switzerland as “another notable neutral” — older (1815) armed neutrality, Outer Seven, no UN membership but UN agencies, quadrilingual federal council system, recognition of Red China.

14. England, the Most Important Country

1936 cast (Baldwin, Chamberlain, Churchill in the wilderness, Macmillan as eccentric back-bencher) against today’s Macmillan–Gaitskell generation; dead titans; Suez killing Eden’s career. England still “the most important country” in Gunther’s ranking of Western Europe — language, law, parliamentary habit, sterling and Commonwealth ties, nuclear and diplomatic weight. Postwar arc: abdication, Munich, war, Attlee’s Labour, Churchill’s return, affluent society and its discontents. Issues displacing 1936’s Hitler–unemployment–India triad: defence, Europe, class and party, the special relationship.

15. Defense and Nuclear Disarmament

Two large English issues that also define new Europe: defence (NATO, US bases, unilateral nuclear disarmament) and the Common Market. Britain as a sitting duck in the missile age — small, concentrated, minutes from East German missiles; ~sixty megaton bombs as a destroy-the-island calculation. Public nuclear dread unlike Italy’s relative silence; fear of both USSR and, on another level, the Pentagon. Limited defence budget (~£1,600 million); independent deterrent ambitions and dependence on American systems; Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament and Labour splits; NATO’s meaning for an island no longer defended by the Channel.

16. Macmillan

Takes office January 1957 at the nadir after Suez — split country, wrecked Anglo-American relations, restive Commonwealth, sterling flight, leaderless Tories. Five years on: wound healed, Washington harmony, record gold stock, Macmillan undisputed party leader — though 1961 erodes prestige (golf-at-Gleneagles shrug during the Berlin crisis). Nicknames from “Mr. MacMothballs” to “Nonstop Mac.” Half-Scot character; evolution with the country’s recovery; style of unflappable superiority. Domestic politics, “never had it so good,” and the turn toward Europe as the next defining gamble.

17. The Establishment and the Opposition

Word “Establishment” unheard by Gunther in a 1958 London stay; ubiquitous by 1960 — Fairlie’s Spectator coinage for an old idea Gunther had called an “inner circle” in 1936 (Baldwin, Dawson, Salisbury, Norman, et al.). Fluidity, not a closed oligarchy: birth, brains, and wealth none sufficient alone. Schools, clubs, Civil Service, City, BBC, court. Conservative governance and Labour Opposition under Hugh Gaitskell; Bevanite aftershocks; class accents in a changing prosperity. How Britain is run when formal democracy and informal networks braid together.

18. A Word on NATO

Fashionable doubts — de Gaulle’s line that NATO was designed for conditions that no longer exist (1949 before Soviet bomb, ICBMs, tactical nukes). Lippmann on peripheral defencelessness once the USSR is nuclear. Original shield concept (stop or delay a Red Army march); never near the planned ninety-six divisions; still some military and large psychological-political value after thirteen peacetime years among fifteen nations. Headaches: cumbersome command (“so few commanded by so many”); unequal contributions; Greece–Turkey Cyprus quarrel; Spain admission question; Portuguese waters; above all France — independent deterrent, US bombers off French soil, Mediterranean fleet withdrawn. Alliance held together by mutual need despite frictions.

19. At Sixes and Sevens

Inner Six (France, FRG, Italy, Benelux) of the EEC / Common Market vs Outer Seven (EFTA: UK, Scandinavia trio, Austria, Switzerland, Portugal). Treaty of Rome mechanics: staged internal tariff cuts (toward zero perhaps by 1966); common external tariff; Hallstein’s Brussels secretariat; trade and production surges; Monnet–Adenauer hope for a United States of Europe. Britain’s 1955 snub, later half-offers to Coal and Steel / Euratom refused unless full EEC entry; Commonwealth trade (~40%) and balance-of-power instinct as barriers. 1961 negotiations as the hinge — British adhesion would be a tremendous forward step; rivalry of the two blocs as the economic face of Western Europe’s unfinished unity.

20. Scandinavia—the Outer Bastions

Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland (and Icelandic notes): democracy, monarchy, and varied cold-war alignments. Norway and Denmark in NATO; Sweden’s armed neutrality (Eisenhower’s “fairly friendly” barb); Finland’s delicate Soviet neighbourhood. Welfare statistics, alcohol policy colour, interlocking dynasties (Gustav VI Adolf; Ingrid of Denmark; Olav V). Outer Seven membership; strategic northern flank; sober societies as “outer bastions” of the open European north against the Baltic Soviet shore.

21. Khrushchev at the Summit

Khrushchev on the world stage — UN performances, shoe and cats passages, “American aggression,” hopes and wreckage of Summit diplomacy after the U-2 and Paris May 1960 collapse. Style as weapon: peasant proverbs, theatrical rage, coexistence rhetoric paired with pressure over Berlin. Reading Soviet aims through public spectacle: prestige, division of the West, recognition of the status quo in Eastern Europe, and bargaining over Germany. Summitry as both personal drama and structural deadlock.

22. More About Mr. K.

Biographical close-up: born 17 April 1894, Kalinovka; miner’s son; disputed late literacy; locksmith/shepherd to Bolshevik, party schools, Stalin Industrial Academy (1929). Triple hats — First Secretary CPSU, Chairman of the Council of Ministers, RSFSR Party Bureau chief. Spelling and pronunciation carnival across languages; “Mr. K.” Domestic de-Stalinizing record and its limits; agricultural obsessions; coexistence doctrine; quarrel with China already audible. Personality as the West’s chief puzzle: not Hitler, but a severe long-range threat who can be obstructionist and “peasant nasty” (UN 1960) without craving war for its own sake.

23. Satellites and Soviet Policy

Tabulation of the European satellite empire (Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, GDR, Hungary, Poland, Rumania — ~96.9 million) plus Yugoslavia in a special category. Ranking by subservience: Czechoslovakia most loyal; then East Germany (Ulbricht; Red Army; Berlin crisis); Rumania; Bulgaria; Hungary (1956 rising; Kádár’s Titoist past); Poland almost not a true satellite; Yugoslavia Communist but neutral outside the fold; Albania quarreling with Moscow and leaning China. Surviving national characters and frictions under Kremlin control. Instruments of Soviet policy — party, army, trade, ideology — and the limits revealed by Belgrade, Warsaw, Tirana, and the memory of Budapest.

24. A Few Paragraphs in Conclusion

Seven staples pulled taut: (1) aged leaders (Adenauer 86, de Gaulle 71, Macmillan 67, Gronchi 74, Franco 69, Tito 69, Khrushchev 67) and an impending generational change; (2) Berlin / German future as the most pressing issue — wall as Europe cut in half in a two-power world; (3) Western alliance frayed (Suez, de Gaulle’s prestige claims, Adenauer’s fear of Anglo-American softness) but intact because neither shore can afford the other’s loss; (4) lineup of blocs and awkward neutrals (Austria, Ireland, Switzerland, Sweden, Finland; Yugoslavia and Albania sui generis; Spain outside NATO); (5) Europe’s fate tied to Afro-Asian and Latin American uncommitted states after imperial retreat; (6) forces of unity — Coal and Steel, Euratom, above all the Common Market; British entry decisive; Charlemagne’s frontiers echoed; a new “Great Power” possibly forming; (7) democratic impulses ascendant in the West — only Franco and Salazar left as Western European dictators. Closes as he opened: general war still unlikely (Khrushchev ≠ Hitler), but miscalculation could mean annihilation — Einstein’s “World War IV — rocks.”
