Content Summary

Préface — La France du temps présent. The volume puts a final point (or a suspension point) to the new Cornette Histoire de France for twenty-first-century readers. It covers a sequence beginning in 1944–1945 and ending about 2005, shortly before the quinquennat of Nicolas Sarkozy (2007) and the world financial crisis that followed. It is the fourth volume of the period called “contemporaine” in the French tradition — a periodisation that begins, in this collection, with the Revolution. The expression “temps présent,” used for about thirty years, designates a period immediately accessible to the historian through the direct testimony of the actors, their living memory and their physical presence; this temporality has changed nature in recent decades because average life expectancy has lengthened, creating proximity with events. The preface also (signed Zancarini-Fournel and Delacroix) explains the book’s structure and its insistence on the longue durée of the postwar years, the critique of the “Trente Glorieuses” myth, and the choice of a chronology beginning in 1944 rather than in the mid-1950s. Henry Rousso contributes a framing text on the volume’s place in the series.

Chapitre I — Lendemains de guerre et reconstruction (1945-1951)

The opening chapter takes up the narrative at the end of 1945, after the election of the first constituent assembly and the unanimous election of General de Gaulle as head of the government. Two events frame the approach: the capitulation of the German army and the establishment of genuine universal suffrage (linked to the 1944 ordonnance on the organisation of powers after the Liberation); events must be read in the “épaisseur de l’histoire.” 8 May 1945, though a moment of joy, is set below the euphoria of the Liberation the year before — the moment clouded by the émeutes of the Constantinois in colonial Algeria and their bloody repression, by the news and vision of the first returning deportees from the Nazi camps, and by the wait for the return of prisoners.

I. Lendemains de guerre : vers une stabilisation politique

Postwar political stabilisation: the electoral communist force in 1946, the invention of the Fourth Republic (1944–1946), the Bayeux discourse (16 June 1946), the tripartisme (January 1946–May 1947), the consultations nationales of 1945–1951, the triumph of parties or of parliamentarians, and the succession of GPRF and Fourth Republic governments.

II. Lendemains de guerre : les réformes de structure

Structural reforms. The French economy in ruins (agricultural and industrial production collapsed); beyond the innovations inherited from the 1930s — the idea of planning — a will to reform structures and capacities. Between the state and the market: nationalisations and the Plan (the Plan’s imperative, the nationalised enterprises); the working of the Marshall Plan; cultural democratisation; the Blum–Byrnes accords.

III. Les ruptures de 1947

The ruptures of 1947: the difficult spring, the departure of the communist ministers (the decree “dismissing” them), the “choc hexagonal” of the Cold War, the postwar European landscape, the great November–December 1947 demonstrations, the colonial question and the war in Indochina.

Chapitre II — La transition « modernisatrice » (années 1950-début des années 1960)

I. « L’état de croissance » (Robert Castel)

On the “Trente Glorieuses”: Jean Fourastié’s expression (1979) for the exceptional growth and “révolution invisible” of 1946–1975. These “glorious” years were generally not lived as such by contemporaries. Growth and inflation as the French equation; demographic “revolution” and immigration; the dynamics of modernisation; a “génération modernisatrice”; grands aménagements du territoire.

II. Une nouvelle société ?

Mass consumption: household equipment in 1966, the consumériste contagion, advertising, credit and distribution; a society at the apogee of its industrial phase (Grenadou, paysan français); a working world increasingly fragmented; salarisation, tertiarisation and feminisation (women in the factories).

III. Le social sans « relais politique » ?

Social demands without a political relay: the introuvable “paix contractuelle”; union membership figures 1945–1995; Nantes 1955 (“cette violence ne nous effraie pas”); the revolt of the independents — poujadisme.

Chapitre III — Les mutations du quotidien et du culturel (années 1950-début des années 1960)

I. Le nouvel empire du privé et du quotidien

The new empire of the private and the everyday. The keenest perception of the new place of objects and household equipment in daily life often came from artists and writers on a negative, mocking register — Boris Vian’s Complainte du progrès (1956), Georges Perec’s Les Choses (1965), the film Mon oncle of Jacques Tati. A “civilisation du loisir”? — the tourist.

II. Familles, sexualités et engagements idéologiques

Families (“Familles, je vous aime…”), marriages, divorces and births outside marriage; the campaign against the 1920 law on abortion; sexualities and representations of the feminine — invisible mutations?; the fraying of ideological and religious affiliations (the end of the prêtres-ouvriers); communist density by department in 1952.

III. L’affirmation de l’impératif culturel

The affirmation of the cultural imperative: education and democratisation in progress (university student numbers 1950–1970), médiatisation, américanisation and mass culture (the success of the “presse populaire,” the radio years); modernités artistiques and intellectual recompositions; the intellectual conjuncture between marxisms and the scientist push; the position of history in 1950.

Chapitre IV — De la IVe à la Ve République

The chapter confronts the gaulliste interpretation of the Fourth Republic as a “régime des partis” and the denunciation of the “système.” Serge Berstein recalls that the Fifth Republic “s’est valorisé en diabolisant son prédécesseur.” Historians today recognise that the Fourth Republic laid the foundations of evolutions the Fifth was to reap; the balance is often presented by opposing the successes of economic modernisation and European construction to the political wanderings (ministerial instability) and the incapacity to resolve the “problème algérien.” Against teleological readings, “la IVe République n’était pas inéluctablement condamnée” (René Rémond). The chapter is structured in three parts:

I. De Pinay à Mendès : la modernisation politique introuvable

The end of the Troisième Force and the return of the right to power; the elections of 17 June 1951; the Council of Europe (1949); the Appel de Stockholm; the great ideological-political quarrel of the Fourth Republic — the CED; threats on the Empire; the end of Indochina; the Empire as the blind spot of the national conscience; cartiérisme.

II. Mendès France, la modernité ambiguë

Pierre Mendès France — radical deputy since 1932, minister of de Gaulle at the Liberation, known for technical competence on economic and financial questions, a “Cassandre” for his language of truth (on Indochina). The strategy for the Geneva conference, the meeting with Zhou Enlai. The “style” Mendès France; the mendésiste diaspora.

III. La fin de la IVe République

The end of the Fourth Republic: the moment of Guy Mollet; the legislative elections of 2 January 1956; the deepening Algerian crisis under the Faure and Mollet governments; the investiture crisis of 1958 and the return of de Gaulle.

Chapitre V — Les années algériennes

After the Toussaint 1954 attacks, the government’s priority — shared by all parties and the opinion — was the re-establishment of order. Mendès France told the Assembly the first duty of the government was “d’affecter tous les moyens nécessaires au rétablissement ou au maintien de l’ordre public.” The “maintien de l’ordre” had become, without saying it, a war. Nearly a million French of European origin lived in Algeria in 1954 (over 80% born in Algeria), cohabiting with nearly nine million “Algériens musulmans” excluded from political rights. The chapter treats the war as the object of symbolic struggles — the terms “pieds-noirs,” “Français musulmans d’Algérie,” “guerre d’Algérie” each carrying contested ideological weight. In Algérie the responsibility of the transition and the maintenance of order is examined closely.

Chapitre VI — Mutations du politique (1958-1968)

13 May 1958 opens a period often reduced to the institutional change inaugurating the Fifth Republic. After the referendum adopting the new Constitution (October 1958), for the November legislative elections de Gaulle chose the uninominal majority two-ballot scrutiny (that of the Third Republic), which had the advantage of weakening the role of parties. The gaullist movements federated in the UNR; the abstention rate reached 23%; the UNR won 17.6% and 198 seats, the Indépendants 20% and 133 seats; de Gaulle was elected President on 21 December 1958 by the college of 80,000 grands électeurs (77% of the suffrages), installed at the Élysée on 8 January 1959 with Michel Debré as Prime Minister.

I. 1958, une rupture politique affirmée

The new institutions; the choice of the majority scrutiny; the weakening of party roles under the new regime.

Chapitre VII — Le moment 1968 et ses traces

Whether 1968 constituted a rupture. The chapter notes the successive questions — did the generalised revolt of youth in May 1968 and the greatest general strike of the twentieth century end a month later in an unqualified electoral victory for de Gaulle? The expression of Arthur Rimbaud, Changer la vie, diffused by Henri Lefebvre then by the situationnistes, was taken up by the socialist party (born of the Épinay congress, 1971) as the title of its 1972 programme. The concept of the “années 1968” integrates the crisis of May–June 1968 into a longer period; the traditional chronology (student crisis until 13 May) is criticised as effacing the wider movement. 1968 is treated as the epicentre of a broad contestation — a galaxy of very diverse social, political and cultural movements — set against the “soulèvement mondial de la jeunesse.”

Chapitre VIII — Crises, réponses politiques et recompositions (1975-2005)

The chapter opens on the crisis: “Les Trente Glorieuses brillent comme la lumière du paradis perdu.” Fourastié’s 1979 book is emblematic of the period of crisis beginning in 1973; the political directors’ consciousness of the durable character of unemployment was tardy (the first measures only in 1977), the crisis first read as conjunctural then as structural. Immigration is constructed as a national problem not from the Front national’s first electoral successes (1983) but ten years earlier, feeding on the occulted traces of the Algerian war and on the crisis. Sub-sections include:

  • I. Perceptions de la crise, reconfiguration de l’emploi et chômage — the visibility of the crisis after October 1973; unemployment mounting from 1975; the TGV; the crisis as “horizon d’attente” (Barre’s December 1978 announcement; the Lorrain steelworkers’ March 1979 demonstrations); redécouvertes de l’entreprise and the political alternance of 1981.
  • Union, désunion de la gauche et alternative politique — the Programme commun (26 June 1972); the 110 propositions of Mitterrand’s 1981 candidacy; the investiture of 21 May 1981; the Mauroy government’s first measures; the Grand Louvre and Mitterrand’s grands travaux.
  • Nouvelle condition salariale et recomposition politique — growth of tertiary employment; unequal unemployment; Chirac elected May 1995 and Juppé; the December 1995 “grève par procuration” and the “Tous ensemble” demonstration; the sans-papiers movement (18 March 1996); the dissolution of April 1997; the “désalignement” of electoral sociology and the decline of the working-class vote (1978–2002).
  • II. Nouveaux modèles familiaux — the “crise de la famille”; women on stage (deep evolution of women’s condition); the famille recomposée; sexualities, a revolution?; feminism of the 1970s; new norms of célibat and marriage; access to parenthood.
  • III. Massification et blocages de l’École — the democratisation of education (Des héritiers?, Bourdieu and Passeron); the “révolution silencieuse” of the baccalauréat; pupils “issus de l’immigration”; the 24 June 1984 demonstration for the private school (over a million participants); the Devaquet law and the 1986 student protests; the growth of the bacheliers proportion and of higher-education students.
  • IV. Villes et banlieues en crise : ethnicisation, genre et identités — the abbé Pierre’s appeal (February 1954); the time of the grands ensembles (14 million dwellings in 1954 for 43 million people); the bidonvilles of the Paris region; the 1973 Guichard circulaire halting the grands-ensembles programme; ségrégations in the banlieue; urban rebellions (Minguettes at Vénissieux; from 1969 the first urban violence); the Marche pour l’égalité (Marseille 15 October 1983–Paris); the “tournant de 1989” — la fin de l’histoire?; the fall of the Wall and German reunification (1990); European stakes since 1989 (Maastricht referendum 1992); Aimé Césaire on identity and the universal; gender and religion in “l’ethnie” (the foulard affair, autumn 1989); the Gulf war (Mitterrand’s allocution of 16 January 1991); the Ni putes ni soumises movement (Fadela Amara, 2003); the Clichy-sous-Bois émeutes (27 October 2005); the Marche des Indigènes de la République (8 May 2005); identity nationale and the extreme right.
  • Conclusion : la France qui bouge — mobility and migrations; the Airbus A380 (2005); France as world tourism capital; the TGV and the viaduc de l’Anguienne, the pont de Normandie; Paris and the coastal/rural resorts (La Grande Motte); mobilités et réseaux (the motorway échangeurs, the Minitel).

Chapitre IX — L’atelier de l’historien

A methodological chapter on the sources of the history of the “temps présent.” The chronological arc (1945 to today) places the book in what is conventionally called contemporary history; the Second World War long served as the “matrice du temps présent,” a concept now questioned. Against received ideas, the potential sources are innumerable — written, printed, sound and iconographic. The chapter examines the limits of written sources (which can seem “rigidifiées,” not transmitting what Philippe Braud calls “dynamiques émotionnelles”), the diversity of archives (state, private, associations, parties, unions), oral and audiovisual sources, and the craft of the historian: cross-referencing sources of different natures, situating knowledge (Michel de Certeau — “les archives ne font que révéler un manque”).

Annexes

  • Repères chronologiques — a chronological frame (in italics the chronology of exterior events) opening in 1944–1945: the ordonnance of 21 April 1944 establishing universal suffrage (vote and eligibility of metropolitan women); 1 September “Bataille de la production”; 9 September new Provisional Government under de Gaulle; the 30 September ordonnance on the press; 15 October foundation of the CGC; 25–26 November constitutive congress of the MRP; 27 November return of Maurice Thorez; publication of Le Monde; 16 January 1945 creation of the Régie Renault (RNUR); 22 February creation of the comités d’entreprise; and so on through to 2005.
  • Notices biographiques — biographical notices of the principal actors.
  • Bibliographie — a full bibliography, including the general works on the period (e.g. Les Idées en France (1945-1988). Une chronologie, Gallimard; “L’Aventure des idées. Chronologie 1989-1999”, Le Débat, n° 111, Septembre-octobre 2000; Guillaume Sylvie, La France contemporaine. Chronologie commentée (1946-1990), 2 tomes, Perrin, 1990; Gerbet Pierre (dir.), Dictionnaire historique de l’Europe unie, 2009; INSEE statistical works; Maitron/Pennetier, Dictionnaire biographique du mouvement ouvrier français), plus the thematic bibliography by chapter.
  • Références iconographiques, sources des textes, cartes, tableaux, graphiques, crédits photographiques — the documentary apparatus of the volume.

Relationship to the Wiki

Zancarini-Fournel gives this wiki its continuous scholarly history of France from 1945 to 2005. It reads alongside — and repeatedly in dialogue with — the de Gaulle Discours et Messages tomes (T2–T5), which supply the French case in the General’s own voice from the Fourth Republic through April 1969. Where de Gaulle provides the first-person statements of the founder of the Fifth Republic, Zancarini-Fournel provides the critical synthesis that situates, contextualises and questions that record (the Fourth Republic’s rehabilitation against the gaulliste “régime des partis” reading; the Trente Glorieuses myth; the années algériennes; May 1968 and its traces; the post-1973 crises and recompositions). It stands beside the two Tusell volumes for Spain as the French pillar of the wiki’s postwar continental coverage.
