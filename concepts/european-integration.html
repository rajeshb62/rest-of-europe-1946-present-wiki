European Integration

European integration is the process by which the states of Europe, above all those of Western and then central and eastern Europe, have bound themselves together since 1945 into an increasingly dense web of shared institutions, policies and law — a process that culminated in the European Union, the most ambitious experiment in the voluntary pooling of sovereignty in modern history. It is at once a historical movement, a legal order, a political system and a set of ideas. It began in the late 1940s with the recovery of a devastated continent and the desire to make war between the European powers impossible; it proceeded through a succession of treaties and enlargements, from the Treaty of Rome to Maastricht and Lisbon; and it created institutions — the European Commission, the Council, the European Parliament, the Court of Justice — that constitute a political system unlike any national one, governing some of the lives of half a billion people without ever becoming a state. The history of European integration is the history of the attempt to answer a single question: whether the nations of Europe, having brought catastrophe upon the world in their rivalries, could learn to govern their interdependence in common. And it is a question that the theorists of integration, and the peoples of Europe themselves, have answered in very different ways.

From the Schuman Plan to the Communities

The decisive founding act came on 9 May 1950, when the French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman, on the advice of the planner Jean Monnet, proposed that France and Germany place their coal and steel industries under a common High Authority. The proposal, addressed to the former hereditary enemy, was the origin of everything that followed: the European Coal and Steel Community of 1951, and then, after the failure of the proposed European Defence Community in 1954, the decision taken at Messina in 1955 to go forward on the economic rather than the military path. The treaty-of-rome-1957 of 1957 established the European Economic Community — a customs union among six states, France, Germany, Italy and the Benelux countries — and Euratom, and set the enterprise on the course of economic integration that was to be its enduring engine. The method, known as the Monnet method or functionalism, was to bind the nations together through common material interests, proceeding step by step and sector by sector, so that war would become not only unthinkable but materially impossible. From these beginnings grew the Community’s distinctive institutions: a Commission to propose and execute policy, a Council of member states to decide, a Parliament to represent the peoples, and a Court to interpret and enforce a law that, by the celebrated rulings of the early 1960s, took direct effect in the legal orders of the member states.

Deepening and widening

The subsequent history of integration is a history of two movements, deepening and widening, which together define the Union’s character. Deepening — the intensification of the common project — proceeded through a series of treaty revisions. The single-european-act-1986 of 1986 relaunched integration after the stagnation of the 1970s, committing the Community to complete its internal market by 1992 and extending qualified-majority voting. The maastricht-treaty-1992 of 1992 transformed the Community into the European Union, adding the single currency and the goal of economic and monetary union, a common foreign and security policy, cooperation on justice and home affairs, and the new citizenship of the Union. The euro-introduction-1999-2002 saw the single currency born in 1999 and the notes and coins of the euro enter circulation in 2002. Widening — the expansion of membership — carried the Community from its six founders to, by the accession of 2007, twenty-seven member states, above all through the eastern-enlargement-2004 of 2004, which reunited the continent after the fall of the Berlin Wall. Each enlargement brought new states, new economic weight and new strains; each deepening brought new competences and new questions of legitimacy. The two movements were never wholly harmonious: the tension between the breadth of the Union and the depth of its integration was to be a permanent theme of its history, and of the crises — the euro-crisis-2010 and brexit-2016 — that tested it in the twenty-first century.

A political system without a state

By the end of the twentieth century the Union had become, in the description of political scientists, a political system in its own right — a “multi-level” polity in which authority was divided between the supranational institutions in Brussels, the national governments, and the regional and local authorities of the member states. Its legal order, built by the Court of Justice, held that Community law took precedence over national law and created rights directly enforceable by individuals. Its institutions, originally technocratic, acquired an increasingly democratic and political character: the European Parliament, directly elected since 1979, gained ever greater powers through successive treaties until it shared legislative authority with the Council on most matters. Yet the Union was not a state, and its members never wished it to become one. It had no government in the national sense, no monopoly of legitimate violence, no final say over the security and identity of its peoples. The editors of the Cambridge History describe it as a political system that governs through the member states — an “inside-out” polity in which the instruments of integration (its money, its policies, its legal culture, its migration regime) shape the continent from within. Whether such a system, lacking the democratic legitimacy of a nation-state, could command the loyalty of its peoples was the question of its age.

Theories of integration

The effort to explain European integration has generated one of the richest bodies of theory in the study of politics, and the theories themselves have shaped the way the process is understood. The earliest grand theory, neofunctionalism, associated with Ernst Haas, argued that integration would proceed through a logic of “spillover” — that integration in one sector would create pressures for integration in adjacent sectors, until a supranational authority acquired a momentum of its own. The rival intergovernmentalist tradition, associated with Stanley Hoffmann, insisted that the nation-state remained the master of the process, and that integration advanced only when national governments chose to advance it. Against both, the American political scientist Andrew Moravcsik proposed liberal-intergovernmentalism, which held that integration was best explained as a series of rational choices by national governments responding to the economic interests of their domestic constituents, to the relative power of states, and to the desire for credible commitments. For Moravcsik the history of the Community, from Messina to Maastricht, was not the work of visionaries or supranational entrepreneurs but the product of converging national economic interests — a claim that located the driving force of integration not in the supranational institutions but in the member states themselves. The debate between these theories was not merely academic: it was a debate about what the Union was, where its power truly lay, and whether its future belonged to the nation-states or to a deeper union.

The Union as a political order

European integration remains, as its historians insist, a work in progress and a question rather than a settled fact. It has been sustained by the great narratives that the Cambridge History traces — of peace, of prosperity, of solidarity, of democracy — and it has been tested by their limits. The peace narrative, built on Franco-German reconciliation, survived the end of the Cold War; the prosperity narrative was strained by the euro-crisis-2010; the narrative of solidarity was tested by the migration crisis; and the promise of democracy faced the persistent complaint that the Union was a technocracy removed from its peoples, a complaint that brexit-2016 carried to its most dramatic conclusion. Yet for all its crises, the Union has proved a durable political order. It has absorbed thirteen new members since its founding six; it has survived the departure of Britain; and in the face of the Russian war in Ukraine it has reasserted the original purpose that had never quite faded — the integration of Europe as the condition of its peace. The process that began with the Schuman Plan has not ended, and it has not become a state. It has instead produced something new in political life: a union of nations that pool their sovereignty without surrendering it, governing their interdependence in common, forever in question and forever in being.
